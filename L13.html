Tuskegee (1932–1972) & The Belmont Report (1979)

How a 40-year ethical failure created the three pillars of modern human-subjects research.
Slide 03
🏛️ Foundation of Modern Bioethics
  • The Tuskegee Syphilis Study: US Public Health Service researchers tracked 399 Black men in Alabama without informed consent and withheld penicillin for decades after it became the cure.
  • The Belmont Report (1979): Established three mandatory ethical principles—Respect for Persons (informed consent), Beneficence (maximize benefit, minimize harm), and Justice (fairness).
  • The Federal Common Rule: Requires every US university and hospital study to pass an Institutional Review Board (IRB) before enrolling patients or collecting DNA.
Core Rule: Informed consent is not a formality—participants must understand risks, voluntary withdrawal, and how samples will be used.

Who Owns Excised Tissue? Moore v. Regents of UC (1990)

Can a patient sue for a share of the profits when their removed cells become a $3B biotech product?
Slide 04
🧬 The $3 Billion Spleen Case
  • The Facts: UCLA doctors removed John Moore's spleen for leukemia treatment, secretly grew his cells into an immortalized cell line—lab-grown cells that divide forever instead of dying—patented as "Mo".
  • Property Claim Rejected: The California Supreme Court ruled patients do NOT own physical tissue once it leaves their body—warning that property rights would paralyze medical research.
  • Fiduciary Duty Upheld: The court ruled doctors must disclose financial or research interests before taking samples (informed consent).
Legal Rule: Once tissue leaves your body, you cannot claim ownership or royalties—your legal protection is disclosure and consent.
Moore v. Regents: What the Court Decided Cal. Sup. Ct. • 1990

Henrietta Lacks & HeLa Cells (1951–2023)

How unconsented cervical cancer cells transformed medicine—and forced a 60-year reckoning on genomic consent.
Slide 05
🧫 Immortal Cells, Unconsented Genome
  • 1951 Biopsy: Doctors at Johns Hopkins took cervical tumor cells from Henrietta Lacks without telling her or her family; they became HeLa, the first immortal human cell line.
  • 2013 Genome Controversy: European researchers published the full HeLa genome online without consulting the Lacks family—revealing heritable disease risks for her living descendants.
  • NIH Agreement & Settlements: The NIH moved HeLa genomic data into controlled access with family oversight, and in 2023–2024 the family settled lawsuits against biotech firms (Thermo Fisher, Ultragenyx).
Key Lesson: Publishing a person's genome sequence exposes living relatives—requiring ongoing governance even decades after death.

Group & Community Consent: Greenberg & Havasupai

What happens when families or Indigenous nations donate DNA for one purpose—and researchers use it for another?
Slide 06
🤝 Broken Promises in Genetic Research
  • Greenberg v. Miami Children's (2003): Families of children with fatal Canavan disease donated blood, tissues, and money to find the gene (`ASPA`)—then the hospital patented it and charged royalties on carrier tests.
  • Havasupai Tribe v. ASU (2010): Tribal members gave blood samples for diabetes research; researchers later used those samples without consent for schizophrenia, inbreeding, and migration studies.
  • Result: Arizona State paid $700,000, returned the remaining blood samples, and triggered strict tribal sovereignty rules for genomic research.
Consent Rule: Broad, vague consent forms destroy community trust—ethical biobanks now use tiered or dynamic consent.

Movement 1 • Gene Patents

AMP v. Myriad (2013): The 9–0 Supreme Court Ruling

Why the Supreme Court split the difference between natural genomic DNA and lab-made cDNA.
Slide 09
⚖️ Product of Nature vs. Lab Invention
  • Genomic DNA (gDNA) = NOT Patentable: Justice Clarence Thomas wrote that isolating a natural gene from a chromosome does not create anything new—so natural DNA belongs to everyone.
  • Complementary DNA (cDNA) = Patentable: cDNA, an exon-only DNA copy synthesized in the lab from mRNA without introns, does not occur in nature and can be patented.
  • Why cDNA Didn't Save Myriad: Clinical sequencing reads a patient's natural gDNA directly—so Myriad could no longer block competing clinical tests.
Supreme Court Rule: Natural gDNA is an unpatentable product of nature; synthetic intron-free cDNA is patent-eligible.
gDNA (Unpatentable) vs. cDNA (Patentable) 9–0 Ruling • 2013
Ref: 569 U.S. 576 (2013), Opinion by Justice C. Thomas BIOL 3111 • GENOMIC ETHICS

Post-Myriad Fallout: Lower Prices vs. Secret Databases

Why losing its gene patent pushed Myriad to hoard something even more valuable: variant data.
Slide 10
🗃️ Trade Secrets Replace Patents
  • Immediate Market Win: Within days of the 2013 ruling, Ambry, GeneDx, and Invitae launched competing BRCA panels—cutting prices from $3,500+ to ~$250–$1,500.
  • The New Monopoly (Data Hoarding): During its 20-year monopoly, Myriad tested 1M+ patients and built a proprietary database of Variants of Uncertain Significance (VUS).
  • Refusing to Share: Myriad stopped sharing variant classifications with NIH's public ClinVar database—meaning a patient tested elsewhere could get an 'uncertain' result that Myriad had already solved.
Policy Insight: Outlawing gene patents opened testing competition, but proprietary VUS trade secrets showed why public data sharing (ClinVar) matters.

The Myth of 'Anonymized' Data: Latanya Sweeney (1997)

Why removing your name and Social Security Number does not hide your identity.
Slide 12
🕵️‍♀️ Three Fields Identify 87% of Americans
  • The 'Anonymous' Release: In 1997, Massachusetts released 'de-identified' hospital visit records for state employees—stripping names and SSNs but keeping ZIP code, birth date, and sex.
  • The Linkage Attack: PhD student Latanya Sweeney bought the Cambridge voter registration list for $20 and matched those three fields—mailing Governor William Weld his own private medical records.
  • The Math: Sweeney proved that 87% of the US population is uniquely identified by just 5-digit ZIP + Gender + Date of Birth.
Privacy Rule: Stripping a name ('de-identification') fails whenever public datasets share quasi-identifiers like ZIP code and birth date.

Identity Recovery from Pooled DNA: Homer et al. (2008)

Even publishing average allele frequencies across 1,000 people leaks who was in the study.
Slide 13
📊 Summary Statistics Are Not Safe
  • Before 2008: NIH posted aggregate GWAS allele-frequency tables (case vs. control averages, with zero individual genomes) openly on the web.
  • The Homer Attack (2008): Nils Homer proved that if you already have a person's genotype (from a cup or clinic), you can test across 10,000+ SNPs whether their DNA shifts the pooled average toward the case or control group.
  • Immediate Policy Shock: Within weeks, the NIH and Wellcome Trust pulled public GWAS summary tables behind dbGaP controlled access.
Mathematical Reality: A person's DNA nudges thousands of SNP averages slightly—creating a detectable statistical fingerprint even in a crowd of 1,000.

Identity Recovery from Public Genomes: Gymrek et al. (2013)

How Yaniv Erlich's lab identified 'anonymous' 1000 Genomes participants using only free public websites.
Slide 14
🧩 Y-Chromosome + Surname = Real Name
  • The Target: Publicly posted, 'de-identified' male genomes in the 1000 Genomes Project (carrying only age and US state metadata).
  • Step 1 — Y-STR Surname Match: Because the Y chromosome passes from father to son just like Western surnames, researchers extracted Y-STR markers and queried free recreational genealogy sites (Ysearch, SMGF) to recover the family last name.
  • Step 2 — Triangulation: Combining Surname + Age + State on public people-search sites narrowed thousands of people down to the exact individual and family.
Breakthrough Lesson: Even without a reference DNA sample, public Y-chromosome genealogy + basic demographics can un-mask anonymous genomes.

Cross-Database Linkage: Linking Police STRs to SNP Chips

Why two completely different DNA technologies are no longer isolated silos.
Slide 15
🔗 Bridging Siloed DNA Databases
  • The Old Assumption: Police CODIS profiles measure only 20 STRs (non-coding repeat counts), while 23andMe/GEDmatch measure 600,000+ SNPs—so people assumed the two systems could never talk.
  • Edge et al. (PNAS, 2017): Showed that because STRs sit inside known SNP haplotype blocks (linkage disequilibrium), a 20-STR profile can be matched to a SNP-chip profile with >90–98% accuracy.
  • Privacy Consequence: A consumer SNP profile can be linked to a forensic STR record—and vice versa—even when they share zero markers in common.
Technical Reality: Neighboring DNA variants travel together in blocks (linkage)—so different DNA tests can still identify the same person.
How STRs and SNPs Link Through Haplotype Blocks Edge et al. • PNAS 2017

Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) DNA & The HIPAA Blind Spot

Why medical privacy laws protect your hospital blood test—but NOT your $99 spit kit.
Slide 16
🏥 Hospital Lab vs. Consumer App
  • What HIPAA Covers: The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act binds only 'covered entities'—hospitals, doctors, health insurers, and their clinical billing partners.
  • What HIPAA Misses: Commercial Direct-to-Consumer (DTC) companies like 23andMe, AncestryDNA, and third-party upload sites are NOT covered by HIPAA.
  • What Protects You Instead: Only the company's Terms of Service and state consumer-privacy laws—which companies can rewrite with an email notice.
Critical Distinction: Your hospital genome is protected by HIPAA; your consumer spit-kit genome is governed by a click-through privacy policy.

Clinical Traps in DTC Tests: False Reassurance & Raw Files

Why a 'negative' consumer BRCA test can give dangerous false reassurance—and why raw .txt uploads misfire.

⚠️ What Consumer Chips Miss
  • The 3-Variant Trap (BRCA1/2): 23andMe's FDA-authorized BRCA report historically tested only 3 Ashkenazi Jewish founder variants—missing >1,000 other pathogenic variants common in the general population.

Return of Secondary Findings: Duty to Warn vs. Right Not to Know

When a doctor sequences a child's genome for epilepsy and accidentally finds an adult cancer risk gene.
Slide 20
🩺 ACMG Secondary Findings (81 Genes)
  • What Are Secondary Findings? When clinical exome/genome sequencing looks for one disease, it also scans 81 medically actionable genes (ACMG SF v3.2)—such as BRCA1, LDLR, MLH1, and RYR1.
  • Duty to Warn (Beneficence): Returning actionable cancer or arrhythmia variants can save a patient's—and their parents'—lives through early screening.
  • Right Not to Know (Autonomy): Patients have the right to opt out of secondary findings, and labs generally avoid testing children for adult-onset, non-actionable diseases like Huntington's.
Clinical Standard: Return medically actionable secondary findings (ACMG 81) by default, while honoring a patient's informed opt-out.

Police CODIS STRs vs. Genealogy SNP Arrays

Why traditional forensic DNA only finds immediate family—while genealogy chips reach 3rd to 5th cousins.
Slide 22
🔬 20 Repeat Markers vs. 600,000+ SNPs
  • CODIS (Combined DNA Index System): Measures just 20 Short Tandem Repeats (STRs)—enough to match a suspect or a parent/sibling, but useless beyond 1st-degree relatives.
  • Genealogy SNP Arrays: Consumer tests (GEDmatch, FamilyTreeDNA) read 600,000+ SNPs across all chromosomes, measuring shared DNA segments in centimorgans (cM).
  • Long-Range Reach: Sharing 50–150 cM reveals 3rd and 4th cousins—connecting a crime-scene sample to people who have never committed a crime or taken a DNA test.
Technical Leap: Moving from 20 CODIS STRs to 600,000 SNPs expanded forensic DNA matching from immediate family to an entire extended family tree.

How Investigative Genetic Genealogy (IGG) Works

The 5-step pipeline that turns an unsolved crime-scene swab into a named suspect.
Slide 23
🌳 From Crime-Scene DNA to Discarded Coffee Cup
  • 1. CODIS Fails → SNP Sequencing: After CODIS finds no match, a private lab generates a 600k+ SNP file from crime-scene DNA.
  • 2. Public Database Upload: Investigators upload the profile to GEDmatch or FamilyTreeDNA to find 3rd–4th cousin matches.
  • 3. Pedigree Triangulation & Confirmation: Genealogists find where two cousin branches intersect (great-great-grandparents), narrow to 1 suspect by age/location, and confirm via a discarded sample (coffee cup/tissue) + CODIS STR match.

GINA (2008): What Federal Law Protects

How the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act shields your health insurance and job.
Slide 29
🛡️ Two Pillars of GINA Protection
  • Title I — Health Insurance: Health insurers cannot raise premiums, deny coverage, or treat a genetic mutation as a 'pre-existing condition' (even before symptoms appear).
  • Title II — Employment (≥15 Staff): Employers cannot use genetic tests or family medical history to hire, fire, promote, or assign duties—and cannot even request your DNA.
  • Broad Definition: GINA protects not just your own DNA test, but also your family medical history and participation in genetic research.
What GINA Covers: Your health insurance and your job (at companies with 15+ employees) are legally protected from genetic discrimination.

Historical Precedent: Asilomar (1975) & Self-Governance

How biologists voluntarily paused recombinant DNA experiments in 1975 to set safety rules.
Slide 32
🔬 The Template for CRISPR Moratoriums
  • The 1973–1974 Alarm: When Paul Berg and Herbert Boyer first cut-and-pasted genes across species (recombinant DNA), scientists feared accidentally creating unstoppable cancer viruses or antibiotic-resistant bacteria.
  • The Asilomar Conference (1975): 140 biologists, lawyers, and journalists met in California, called a voluntary moratorium, and created tiered biosafety containment levels (BSL-1 to BSL-4).
  • Legacy: Asilomar proved scientific self-regulation can earn public trust—and became the model for modern CRISPR germline summits.

Movement 4 • Gene Editing Ethics

Somatic vs. Germline Gene Editing

Why FDA-approved CRISPR cures like Casgevy are celebrated—while embryo editing remains banned.
Slide 33
🧬 One Patient vs. Every Future Descendant
  • Somatic Gene Editing: Edits non-reproductive cells (e.g., bone marrow stem cells in Casgevy for Sickle Cell Disease). Changes stay inside one consenting patient and die with them.
  • Germline Gene Editing: Edits sperm, eggs, or early embryos—altering every cell in the child's body and passing edits to all future generations.
  • Consent Problem: An embryo—and its great-grandchildren—cannot consent to permanent, irreversible DNA edits or off-target mutations.
Ethical Line: Somatic editing treats a sick individual with consent; germline editing permanently alters the human gene pool.

Case Study: The He Jiankui CRISPR Babies (2018)

Why secretly editing twin embryos for HIV resistance violated every scientific and ethical rule.
Slide 34
🚫 Five Ethical & Scientific Violations
  • What Happened: In November 2018, biophysicist He Jiankui announced the birth of twin girls (“Lulu” and “Nana”) whose CCR5 gene he edited as IVF embryos to mimic HIV resistance.
  • No Medical Need: The father was HIV+, which is already 100% preventable in IVF via simple sperm washing—there was zero reason to risk experimental embryo editing.
  • Botched Science & Coerced Consent: The edits created novel, untested CCR5 deletions and mosaicism (some cells edited, some not), ethics approvals were forged, and He was sentenced to 3 years in prison.
Lesson: Even when a technical tool (CRISPR) works in a dish, premature human experimentation without medical necessity, safety, or oversight is unethical.

Algorithmic Equity: When Genomic Models Fail Diverse Patients

Why biased training cohorts turn precision medicine into unequal medicine.
