Structural Variants, Gene Dosage & Cytogenetic Diagnostics

How submicroscopic deletions, duplications, and dosage imbalance cause human disease.

The Physical Scale of Genomic Rearrangements

Single nucleotide variants alter individual base pairs. In contrast, structural variants (SVs)—genomic rearrangements involving 50 or more base pairs—alter large contiguous chromosomal segments through deletion, duplication, inversion, or translocation.

In clinical genetics, structural rearrangements alter gene copy number rather than protein primary sequence. Submicroscopic copy number variants account for approximately 15% of causal diagnoses in unexplained neurodevelopmental delay and congenital anomalies.

Clinical Diagnostic Context: Chromosomal microarrays identify pathogenic copy number variants in ~15–20% of children with unexplained neurodevelopmental delay who have normal routine karyotypes.

Media Headlines vs. Biomarker Science

How public reporting conflated statistical risk shifting with deterministic disease onset.

Analytical Audit of Public Claims

  • Predictive Exaggeration: News outlets reported that plasma p-tau217, a phosphorylated tau protein fragment measured in circulating blood, could pinpoint the exact calendar year a patient would develop dementia.
  • Misinterpreting Hazard Ratios: Reports treated a hazard ratio, a relative metric comparing the rate of cognitive decline between biomarker groups over time, as an absolute individual death sentence.
  • Ignoring Pre-Test Probability: Commentators urged testing in healthy adults, ignoring how low disease prevalence drives high false-positive rates.
Diagnostic Caveat: Circulating biomarkers track current protein accumulation, not an unalterable calendar date of future cognitive impairment.

Study of the Week • Clinical Translation & Population Validity

Reasonable Inferences: Clinical Translation & Limitations

Distinguishing what Xu et al. actually established from unjustified clinical extrapolation.

What Was Scientifically Established

  • Synergistic Risk Factor: Plasma p-tau217 strongly correlates with cognitive decline over an 8-year follow-up, and APOE ε4 significantly amplifies this relationship.
  • Multi-Ancestry Replicability: The biomarker-genotype interaction was observed across Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, and non-Hispanic White cohorts.
  • Enrichment for Clinical Trials: Combining p-tau217 with APOE genotyping identifies high-risk cohorts for preventative anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody trials.
Clinical Trial Utility: Stratifying clinical trial participants by p-tau217 and APOE status enriches cohorts for rapid converters, reducing necessary sample sizes.

What CANNOT Be Reasonably Inferred

  • No Diagnostic Destiny: Elevated p-tau217 does not guarantee cognitive impairment; many high-tau individuals remained cognitively intact throughout the 8-year window.
  • Asymptomatic Screening Invalid: Routine blood testing in general population clinics remains unrecommended because low disease prevalence produces substantial false-positive burdens.
  • No Monotherapy Decision: Fluid biomarkers reflect pathology but cannot dictate medical decisions without comprehensive clinical memory testing and neuroimaging.
Population Health Note: Validating biomarker cutoffs across diverse community settings is required before routine clinical implementation to avoid misestimating risk.
Clinical Translation: Fluid biomarkers are valuable prognostic stratification tools for research, but they do not replace formal clinical assessment. TRANSLATIONAL REALITY →
Movement I • Genomic Architecture

The Genomic Spectrum: Physical Scale of Alterations

Human genetic variation spans from single nucleotides to whole chromosomes.

Classification by Physical Size

  • Single Nucleotide Variants (1 bp): Alter single base pairs; most numerous (~4–5 million per genome), altering single amino acids or regulatory motifs.
  • Small InDels (1–49 bp): Insertions or deletions of 1 to 49 base pairs; frequently cause reading frameshifts or discrete amino acid loss.
  • Structural Variants (≥50 bp): Large genomic rearrangements—including copy number variants, inversions, and translocations—that alter chromosomal architecture.
Physical Base Pair Impact: Although less numerous than SNVs, structural variants alter more cumulative base pairs in every human genome.
SELECTED: CNV / Structural Variant (≥50 bp) Alters gene copy number or disrupts coding boundaries
Scale Takeaway: Structural variants alter gene copy number and genomic topology rather than individual amino acid letters. EXPLORE SCALE →
Movement I • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: What Can an SV Do to a Gene?

When chromosomal segments break, move, or rejoin, genes face four physical fates.

The Four Physical Outcomes for a Gene

  • Whole-Gene Deletion (Loss): The structural variant physically removes the entire gene or its promoter; copy number drops from two to one (or zero), cutting functional protein synthesis in half.
  • Whole-Gene Duplication (Gain): The structural variant inserts an extra copy of the complete gene; copy number increases from two to three, flooding the cell with excess wild-type protein.
  • Intragenic Disruption (Truncation): An SV breakpoint lands directly inside an exon or intron; the coding frame is severed, creating a truncated or completely inactive protein fragment.
  • Gene Fusion (Hybrid Chimera): Breakpoints join severed pieces of two different genes; the hybrid transcript fuses two functional domains into a novel chimeric oncoprotein (e.g., BCR-ABL1).
The Critical Mechanism Distinction: Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) change a single letter inside a gene. Structural variants either change how many normal copies exist (dosage) or physically shatter and fuse gene structures.
Four Physical Fates: A structural variant alters a gene in four fundamental ways: deleting it, duplicating it, cutting it in half, or fusing it to another gene. PHYSICAL GENE FATES →
Movement I • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: Gene Copies, Protein Quotas & Pathways

How diploid gene copies translate into stoichiometric quotas inside cellular machines.

From Chromosomes to Functional Modules

  • The Diploid Quota (2 Copies = 100%): Every somatic cell carries two copies of each autosome (one maternal, one paternal). Each gene copy produces a defined quota of messenger RNA, generating the calibrated protein dosage required for health.
  • Proteins Form Functional Modules: Proteins rarely operate in isolation; they physically dock together to build macromolecular complexes, multi-subunit protein machines that catalyze biochemical reactions or maintain cellular structure.
  • The Pathway Bottleneck ('Nerfing the Chain'): Multi-protein complexes require strict stoichiometry, an exact ratio of interacting parts (e.g., 1:1 ratio). Nerfing one subunit by 50% or overproducing it by 150% stalls the entire assembly chain, causing deficiency or toxic proteotoxic jams.
Macroscopic Analogy: Like an automotive assembly line: if the engine factory slows by 50% or surges by 150%, incomplete cars accumulate and the factory floor halts.
The Stoichiometric Law: Cellular health depends on balanced protein ratios; altering gene copy number disrupts multi-protein complexes and cascades across entire biochemical pathways. PATHWAY STOICHIOMETRY →
Movement I • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: Classic Stoichiometry & Pathways

How physical subunit ratios and enzymatic cascades turn genetic dosage into human disease.

Landmark Complexes & The Pathway Bucket Brigade

  • Fixed Molecular Stoichiometry (α2β2 Hemoglobin): Functional hemoglobin requires exactly two α-globin and two β-globin subunits (2:2 ratio). Losing one β-globin gene leaves unpartnered orphan α-chains that precipitate into insoluble toxic crystals, destroying red blood cells (β-Thalassemia).
  • Coupled Molecular Machines (The 1:1 Ribosome): Protein translation requires equal numbers of small (40S) and large (60S) ribosomal subunits. Cutting production of either subunit leaves its partner uncoupled and idle, throttling global cellular protein synthesis by 50%.
  • The Multi-Step Pathway (Bucket Brigade): Biochemical reactions operate in sequential chains where Enzyme 1 feeds Enzyme 2. Halving the dosage of a single enzyme creates two clinical consequences at once: a toxic upstream substrate jam and downstream end-product starvation.
The Metabolic Bottleneck Rule: A multi-protein machine is only as functional as its scarcest subunit, and a metabolic pathway is only as fast as its slowest enzymatic step.
Stoichiometry & Pathways: Genes do not act alone; altering copy number creates unpartnered toxic proteins or chokes multi-step biochemical cascades. CLASSIC STOICHIOMETRY →
Movement I • Stoichiometric Assembly

Gene Dosage & Stoichiometry: Complex Assembly

Why diploid cells require balanced expression of interacting protein subunits.

The Principle of Stoichiometric Balance

  • Protein Subunits: A subunit, an individual protein chain that docks with partner proteins to build a multi-protein machine, requires strictly balanced production.
  • Stoichiometric Assembly: Multi-protein complexes demand exact equimolar ratios; producing subunits out of balance leaves excess individual chains without binding partners.
  • Ubiquitin Tagging & Toxicity: Ubiquitin, a small molecular tag that marks surplus or misfolded proteins for proteasomal destruction, labels orphaned subunits until disposal machinery jams.
Ubiquitin Pool Depletion: Excess orphan subunits deplete the cell's free ubiquitin pool, causing unpartnered proteins to aggregate and trigger proteotoxic cell death.
Subunits & Ubiquitin: When excess subunits fail to dock into complexes, they exhaust cellular ubiquitin tags and precipitate into toxic aggregates. STOICHIOMETRY →
Movement I • Triplosensitivity

Triplosensitivity: PMP22 Duplication in CMT1A

How a 50% increase in wild-type PMP22 protein impairs peripheral nerve myelination.

PMP22 Normal Biology & Dosage Overload

  • Normal PMP22 Function: Peripheral Myelin Protein 22 (PMP22) is an essential tetraspan transmembrane glycoprotein produced by Schwann cells; it physically stabilizes compact peripheral myelin to ensure rapid nerve impulse conduction.
  • Genomic Architecture (17p12): Located on chromosome 17p12, PMP22 spans ~35.5 kb with 6 exons (5 protein-coding, 2 alternative promoters) and 5 intervening introns, producing a 160-amino-acid (22 kDa) membrane protein.
  • Triplosensitive CMT1A Pathology: A recurrent 1.4 Mb duplication adds a 3rd wild-type PMP22 copy; a 150% protein load overwhelms Schwann cell endoplasmic reticulum quality control, causing demyelinating motor neuropathy.
Clinical Presentation (CMT1A): Patients present in adolescence with distal muscle weakness, foot drop, high foot arches (pes cavus), and slowed motor conduction velocities (<38 m/s).
PMP22 Dosage Sensitivity: Normal PMP22 compacts peripheral myelin; a 50% surplus of unmutated PMP22 overloads Schwann cells, destroying myelin and slowing nerve conduction. CMT1A TRIPLOSENSITIVITY →

Movement I • Haploinsufficiency

Haploinsufficiency: PMP22 Deletion in HNPP

How a 50% reduction in PMP22 produces mechanical nerve vulnerability.

PMP22 Deletion & Tomaculous Neuropathy

  • Reciprocal 1.4 Mb Deletion: The same unequal meiotic crossover that duplicates PMP22 produces a reciprocal 1-copy deletion allele.
  • Compromised Myelin Compaction: Haploinsufficiency, where a single functional copy yields 50% protein dosage that cannot maintain myelin tensile strength under physical strain, causes mechanical instability.
  • Tomacula Formation: Myelin develops focal sausage-like swellings (tomacula) that tear and trigger conduction block under minor compression.
Clinical Presentation: Patients develop transient focal sensory loss or motor palsy after everyday nerve compression (e.g., resting on elbows or crossing legs).
Dosage Comparison: CMT1A (3 copies) produces progressive distal weakness; HNPP (1 copy) produces episodic, pressure-induced conduction block. HAPLOINSUFFICIENCY →

Movement I • The Dosage Paradox

Resolving the Paradox: Why 150% Protein Causes Demyelination

How excess wild-type protein triggers the Unfolded Protein Response in Schwann cells.

Endoplasmic Reticulum Stress & Glial Apoptosis

  • Proteostasis Limitations: Students often assume having more wild-type protein should make thicker myelin; however, cellular proteostasis has strictly finite capacity.
  • ER Folding Bottleneck: The endoplasmic reticulum (ER), the cellular organelle that folds and quality-checks newly synthesized membrane proteins, faces a natural bottleneck. Over 80% of normal PMP22 misfolds and degrades even in healthy cells.
  • Unfolded Protein Response (UPR): A 50% protein surplus saturates ER chaperones, triggering the unfolded protein response (UPR), a cellular stress pathway that halts translation and induces Schwann cell apoptosis.
Pathological Mechanism: CMT1A demyelination occurs because Schwann cells die from internal proteotoxic stress before excess PMP22 ever reaches the myelin sheath.
Paradox Resolution: Excess wild-type protein causes disease by saturating ER folding chaperones, triggering intracellular stress and glial apoptosis. PROTEOSTASIS →
Movement I • Somatic Amplification

Somatic Gene Amplification: ERBB2 (HER2) in Breast Cancer

How focal amplifications drive receptor overexpression and ligand-independent oncogenic signaling.

ERBB2 Biology & Receptor Overexpression

  • ERBB2 Identity & Normal Biology: ERBB2 (HER2), located on chromosome 17q12, encodes a 185 kDa transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinase belonging to the epidermal growth factor receptor family. Normally, it coordinates cell growth and survival during tissue development.
  • Focal Amplification & Dimerization: Uniquely, HER2 lacks a known direct ligand, remaining in an active conformation. In ~20% of breast cancers, focal amplification creates 20–50 copies, driving surface receptor density from 20,000 to over 2 million. Receptors collide and homodimerize spontaneously, driving unchecked cell division.
  • Targeted Monoclonal Blockade: Trastuzumab (Herceptin), a humanized monoclonal antibody, binds the HER2 extracellular domain (subdomain IV). It sterically prevents dimerization, accelerates receptor degradation, and recruits immune cells to destroy tumor cells.
Diagnostic Stratification (IHC & FISH): Pathologists evaluate ERBB2 status via immunohistochemistry (protein 3+) and FISH (HER2/CEP17 ratio ≥2.0) to select candidates for targeted Trastuzumab therapy.
Therapeutic Principle: High-level oncogene amplification creates both a growth driver and a selective molecular target for monoclonal antibody therapy. TARGETED THERAPY →
Movement I • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: Meiotic Synapsis & Homology

How chromosomes align by matching sequence identity, and how regional repeats trigger misalignment.

The Chromosomal Pairing Engine

  • The Meiotic Imperative: To generate haploid sperm or egg cells, maternal and paternal chromosomes must find each other, align side-by-side in synapsis, and exchange genetic segments via crossing over.
  • The Sequence Search Engine: Chromosomes align as specialized cellular enzymes physically scan for matching stretches of identical base pairs (sequence homology) to connect homologous pairs.
  • Low-Copy Repeat Misalignment: When a chromosome contains duplicate segments (low-copy repeats) separated by millions of base pairs, the homology search machinery can mispair them, triggering unequal crossover.
Structural Susceptibility: Homology search enzymes cannot distinguish between identical repeats at different chromosomal loci, making repetitive blocks prone to unequal exchange.
The Synapsis Principle: Meiotic alignment enzymes pair chromosomes by matching sequence identity; high-identity segmental duplications promote non-allelic pairing that creates microdeletions and microduplications. HOMOLOGY SEARCH →
Movement I • Recombination Mechanics

Meiotic Recombination Mechanics: NAHR

How paralogous low-copy repeats misalign during meiotic synapsis.

Low-Copy Repeats & Unequal Crossing Over

  • Low-Copy Repeats (LCRs): Low-copy repeats (LCRs), large duplicate DNA blocks sharing >98% sequence identity across tens of kilobases, flank dosage-sensitive genes and serve as mispairing substrates.
  • Synaptic Mispairing: During meiotic prophase I, homologous pairing enzymes mistakenly align the distal repeat of one homolog with the proximal repeat of its partner.
  • Reciprocal Crossover (NAHR): Non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) within these misaligned repeats transfers DNA unequally, generating one duplication chromatid and one reciprocal deletion chromatid.
Reciprocal Lesions: Because both products originate from the exact same meiotic mispairing event, CMT1A (duplication) and HNPP (deletion) share identical 1.4 Mb breakpoints.
Mechanistic Takeaway: Pre-existing low-copy repeats in the reference genome provide the homologous substrates for recurrent meiotic mispairing. RECOMBINATION →
Movement I • Meiotic Segregation

Meiotic Segregation: The Fertilization Math

Why an unequal crossover event produces balanced and unbalanced gametes in equal proportions.

Four Chromatid Segregation Pattern

  • Haploid Gamete Segregation: Because gametes are haploid (normally carrying 1 copy of each chromosome), an unequal crossover yields four sperm/egg possibilities: one duplication (2 copies), one deletion (0 copies), and two normal gametes (1 copy each).
  • The Fertilization Math ($2 + 1 = 3$): When an abnormal duplication gamete (2 copies) fertilizes a normal partner gamete (1 copy), the resulting zygote inherits 3 copies of PMP22 ($2 + 1 = 3$), causing CMT1A.
  • The Reciprocal Deletion ($0 + 1 = 1$): Conversely, when the reciprocal deletion gamete (0 copies) fertilizes a normal gamete (1 copy), the child inherits only 1 copy ($0 + 1 = 1$), causing HNPP.
Fertilization Equation: Gamete (2 Copies) + Partner (1 Copy) = 3 Copies (CMT1A). Gamete (0 Copies) + Partner (1 Copy) = 1 Copy (HNPP).
Segregation Principle: Unequal crossing over generates reciprocal products: each deletion gamete (0 copies) corresponds to a matching duplication gamete (2 copies). FERTILIZATION MATH →
Movement I • Genomic Architecture

Recurrent Microdeletions: Architectural Hotspots

Why non-allelic homologous recombination occurs repeatedly at predictable chromosomal loci.

Low-Copy Repeats & Architectural Hotspots

  • Paralogous Repeat Architecture: Certain genomic intervals are flanked by low-copy repeats (LCRs) spanning 10 to 500 kb with >98% sequence identity, which frequently mispair during meiotic synapsis.
  • The 22q11.2 Hotspot Architecture: On chromosome 22q11.2, four segmental repeat blocks (LCR22A, B, C, D) flank a 3.0 Mb critical interval; unequal crossing over between LCR22A and LCR22D accounts for ~85% of 22q11.2 microdeletions.
  • The Genome-Wide Hotspot Catalog: Identical architecture drives recurrent microdeletions across the human genome, including 7q11.23 (Williams syndrome), 15q11-q13 (Prader-Willi/Angelman), and 17p11.2 (Smith-Magenis).
Invariant Genomic Breakpoints: Because mispairing occurs specifically between flanking LCR blocks, unrelated patients with 22q11.2 deletion syndrome share identical 3.0 Mb genomic deletion boundaries.
Genomic Architecture: Submicroscopic deletion hotspots arise at predictable loci flanked by high-identity low-copy repeats that promote non-allelic meiotic pairing. HOTSPOT ARCHITECTURE →

Movement I • Genomic Spectrum & Disease Burden

Clinical Distribution: The Microdeletion & CNV Disease Spectrum

Surveying the epidemiological burden, multisystem reach, and unclassified variants of structural DNA changes.

Epidemiological Scope & Clinical Heterogeneity

  • Cumulative Population Burden: While individual syndromes are rare, pathogenic copy number variants (CNVs), structural gains or losses of genomic DNA segments larger than 50 base pairs, collectively affect 1 in 200 live births.
  • Multisystem Clinical Variety: Dosage imbalances disrupt organ systems throughout the body. Manifestations range from congenital heart disease (22q11.2) and aortic stenosis (7q11.23) to morbid obesity (15q11-q13), peripheral neuropathy (17p12), and adult schizophrenia.
  • The Unclassified Genomic Frontier: Most clinical findings are not textbook recurrent syndromes. Clinical microarrays frequently uncover variants of uncertain significance (VUS), newly identified genomic changes where pathogenicity is ambiguous due to variable penetrance, the phenomenon where identical variants cause different disease severities.
Diagnostic Impact: CNVs explain ~15% of children with unexplained developmental delay. Identifying a pathogenic deletion immediately guides targeted cardiac, renal, and neurodevelopmental clinical care.
Clinical Breadth: Pathogenic CNVs span every major organ system, while unclassified variants and variable penetrance represent the primary diagnostic frontier. CNV SPECTRUM →
Movement II • Diagnostic Cytogenetics

Movement II: The Cytogenetic Diagnostic Toolset

Navigating the technological shift from light microscopy to single-nucleotide resolution.

Evolution of Clinical Chromosome Analysis

  • The Diagnostic Dilemma: Clinical geneticists must match the diagnostic testing platform to the physical scale of the suspected genetic defect.
  • Light Microscopy Limitations: For decades, G-banded karyotyping, staining metaphase chromosomes with Giemsa dye to view banding patterns under a light microscope, served as the primary clinical standard but could not detect rearrangements below 5 million base pairs.
  • Molecular Cytogenetics: Modern laboratories utilize fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and chromosomal microarrays (CMA) to resolve submicroscopic rearrangements across the genome.
Used to Diagnose: First-Tier Pediatric Workup: Chromosomal microarray is used to diagnose unexplained developmental delay and congenital anomalies, yielding a causal CNV in 15–20% of cases compared to only 3% by karyotyping.
Diagnostic Scope: Clinical genetic diagnosis requires selecting a platform whose physical resolution matches the suspected chromosomal lesion. DIAGNOSTIC HIERARCHY →

Foundational Concept: DNA Hybridization & Molecular Probes

The physical base-pairing mechanism that transforms invisible genomic copies into visible light.

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH)

  • The Submicroscopic Challenge: DNA double helices are only two nanometers wide—completely invisible under clinical light microscopes. To detect missing or extra genes, we exploit the thermodynamic laws of DNA base pairing.
  • Denaturation & Hybridization: When heated, double-stranded DNA unzips into single strands (denaturation); when cooled, complementary single strands spontaneously seek each other out and reform base pairs (hybridization).
  • The Molecular Probe Mechanism: A molecular probe is a synthetic, single-stranded DNA fragment linked to a bright fluorescent dye molecule. Flood patient DNA with probes: they bind only to their exact matching sequence, where fluorescent intensity equals copy number.
Assay Foundation: Targeted FISH, array CGH, and SNP microarrays all rely on this identical physical principle: fluorescent signal brightness directly reports genomic copy number.
The Hybridization Rule: Molecular cytogenetics does not read DNA base-by-base; it measures the physical brightness of fluorescent probes hybridized to complementary target sequences. PROBE HYBRIDIZATION →

  • Cell Culture & Mitotic Arrest: Peripheral blood lymphocytes are cultured, stimulated to divide with phytohemagglutinin (a mitogen plant lectin), and arrested in metaphase using colchicine (a spindle-poison drug that halts chromosome separation).

Movement II • Data Interpretation

Interpreting Microarray Data: The Log2 Ratio Plot

How clinical cytogeneticists identify deletions and duplications from probe intensity shifts.

Mathematical Principles of Log2 Ratios

  • Baseline Diploid Expectation: Normal genomic regions have 2 copies in patient and 2 copies in control reference; $\log_2(\text{patient}/\text{reference}) = \log_2(2/2) = \mathbf{0.0}$.
  • Heterozygous Deletion Shift: Losing one copy leaves 1 patient copy against 2 reference copies; $\log_2(1/2) = -1.0$; this shift is used to diagnose microdeletions like DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2).
  • Heterozygous Duplication Shift: Gaining an extra copy yields 3 patient copies against 2 reference copies; $\log_2(3/2) = +0.58$; this shift is used to diagnose microduplications like CMT1A (17p12).
Used to Diagnose: Log2 Intensity Shifts: A consecutive run of 20+ probe dots shifting to +0.58 at 17p12 is used to diagnose the 1.4 Mb CMT1A duplication, while downward shifts to -1.0 diagnose the 22q11.2 DiGeorge deletion.
Quantitative Readout: Microarrays plot normalized fluorescence as log2 ratios: deviations to +0.58 diagnose duplications, while shifts to -1.0 diagnose deletions. LOG2 SCALING →
Movement II • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: Diploid Genotypes & Allelic Balance

How counting polymorphic nucleotide letters (A and B) exposes copy number changes and LOH.

The Allelic Ratio Principle

  • What is an Allele? Across our genome, millions of nucleotide positions differ between individuals; we define these as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), arbitrarily labeling the two alternative bases as Allele A and Allele B.
  • The Normal Diploid Triad: Because every somatic cell has two chromosomes, each person possesses exactly one of three diploid genotypes at any SNP: homozygous AA (0% B), heterozygous AB (50% B), or homozygous BB (100% B).
  • Clinical CNV Signatures: In DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2 deletion), losing one copy makes heterozygosity impossible—the middle AB track completely vanishes. In CMT1A (17p12 duplication), an extra copy splits the middle track into two distinct bands: AAB (33%) and ABB (67%).
Used to Diagnose: CNV Allelic Imbalance: Allelic ratios are used to diagnose microdeletions and duplications: a vanishing middle band confirms DiGeorge syndrome (22q11.2), while split 0.33/0.67 bands confirm CMT1A (17p12).
The BAF Principle: Tracking the ratio of B-alleles to total alleles (B / [A+B]) reveals chromosomal deletions, duplications, and copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity without sequencing. ALLELIC BALANCE →
Movement II • SNP Genotyping

B-Allele Frequency (BAF): Genotype Tracking

How allele-specific probe ratios reveal allelic balance and copy-neutral pathology.

Deciphering the Three-Track BAF Plot

  • What is BAF?: B-Allele Frequency (BAF) quantifies the proportion of fluorescent signal generated by the alternative 'B' nucleotide allele at a polymorphic single-nucleotide site: $\text{BAF} = B / (A + B)$, ranging from 0.0 to 1.0.
  • Normal Diploid Triad: Normal diploid regions exhibit three distinct horizontal bands: homozygous $AA$ (0.0), heterozygous $AB$ (0.5), and homozygous $BB$ (1.0).
  • The Duplication Split in CMT1A: Across the 17p12 CMT1A duplication, an extra copy splits the middle heterozygous band into two distinct tracks ($AAB$ at 0.33 and $ABB$ at 0.67), producing four distinct bands ($0, 0.33, 0.67, 1.0$).
Used to Diagnose: Duplications Verified by BAF Split: BAF plots are used to diagnose true copy number duplications—such as 17p12 in CMT1A—by confirming the heterozygous track splits into two bands (0.33 and 0.67), ruling out false chip noise.
Diagnostic Monopoly: B-allele frequency plots provide orthogonal genetic verification: while chip noise causes false log2 spikes, only genuine duplications split the heterozygous band. GENOTYPE VALIDATION →
Movement II • Copy-Neutral Pathology

Copy-Neutral LOH: Uniparental Disomy & Consanguinity

How SNP arrays detect genetic disease when total gene copy number is completely normal.

The Invisible Pathology of CN-LOH

  • Normal Copy Number (Log2 = 0.0): Copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (CN-LOH) occurs when a cell possesses two normal copies of a chromosome, but both are inherited from the same parent.
  • Disappearance of Heterozygous Track: Because all alleles are homozygous ($AA$ or $BB$), the middle BAF band (0.5) completely vanishes across an entire chromosome or segment.
  • Used to Diagnose Imprinting Disorders: SNP arrays are used to diagnose uniparental disomy (UPD), which triggers disorders of genomic imprinting such as Prader-Willi (maternal UPD15) and Angelman syndrome (paternal UPD15).
Used to Diagnose: Uniparental Disomy & Imprinting Disorders: SNP microarrays are used to diagnose Prader-Willi and Angelman syndromes caused by UPD15, which are completely invisible on karyotype and array CGH.
Diagnostic Monopoly: Copy-neutral LOH maintains normal diploid copy number while obliterating heterozygosity, providing the diagnostic signature for uniparental disomy. UNIPARENTAL DISOMY →

Technical Limitation of Microarrays: Balanced Rearrangements

Why oligonucleotide hybridization cannot detect chromosome translocations or inversions.

The Physics of DNA Quantity vs. Genomic Address

  • Hybridization Counts Fragments: Microarrays measure relative DNA quantity by counting how many fluorescent fragments bind to immobilized probe sequences.
  • Zero Net Change in DNA: In a balanced reciprocal translocation, segments of two chromosomes swap places without any net gain or loss of genetic material.
  • The Diagnostic Dilemma: A patient carrying a balanced reciprocal translocation will have a completely NORMAL chromosomal microarray report despite high risk for recurrent miscarriages.
Used to Diagnose: Parental Balanced Swaps in Infertility: Karyotyping is used to diagnose parental balanced translocations in couples with recurrent pregnancy loss, where microarrays yield a falsely reassuring 'normal' result.

Pathogenic Classification Criteria (>99% Certainty)

ACMG Tier 2: Likely Pathogenic CNVs

High-confidence disease variants with compelling biological evidence but fewer published human cases.

ACMG Tier 3: Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS)

The diagnostic gray zone where genomic evidence is insufficient to confirm or rule out disease causality.

VUS Criteria (The Inconclusive 10–90% Zone)

  • Inconclusive Evidence: A Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS) carries contradictory or insufficient data, falling squarely between 10% and 90% diagnostic certainty.

ACMG Tier 4: Likely Benign CNVs

Variants with strong evidence of harmlessness that do not disrupt critical protein-coding sequences.

Likely Benign Criteria (90–99% Harmless)

  • Low-Frequency Control Matches: The CNV is detected in healthy adult control cohorts (gnomAD-SV or DGV) at low to moderate frequencies between 0.1% and 1.0%.

ACMG Tier 5: Benign CNVs

Common population polymorphisms present in healthy humans that carry zero clinical risk.

Benign Classification Criteria (>99% Harmless)

  • Common Population Polymorphism: A structural variant is classified as Benign if its frequency equals or exceeds 1.0% in healthy population databases like gnomAD-SV.

Movement III • Foundational Concept

Foundational Concept: Circulating cfDNA & Cellular Turnover

Why apoptotic cell death releases fragmented maternal and placental genomes into peripheral blood.

Apoptosis & Plasma Liquid Biopsy

  • Programmed Cell Death (Apoptosis): In healthy human tissues, billions of aged or damaged cells undergo apoptosis daily. Caspase-activated endonucleases cleave cellular chromatin specifically between protective histone octamers into discrete nucleosomal fragments.
  • Shedding into Plasma: Instead of degrading inside the cell, these tiny fragments (~140 to 170 base pairs long) escape into interstitial fluid and enter bloodstream capillaries as naked cell-free DNA (cfDNA).
  • The Maternal-Placental Cocktail: During pregnancy, the placenta's outer layer (syncytiotrophoblast) continuously sheds apoptotic fragments directly into maternal blood, creating a mixed plasma cocktail of ~90% maternal DNA and ~10% placental fetal-genotype DNA.
Cell-Free DNA Biology: A simple routine maternal blood draw samples billions of freely floating placental DNA fragments, eliminating the procedural miscarriage risk of invasive uterine needles.
Cell-Free Mechanism: Non-invasive prenatal testing does not capture intact fetal cells; it sequences a natural cocktail of apoptotic nucleosomal DNA fragments freely floating in maternal plasma. PLASMA LIQUID BIOPSY →
Movement III • Historical Breakthrough

The Discovery: Circulating Cell-Free Fetal DNA

How Dennis Lo established that fetal DNA fragments circulate in maternal bloodstream.

The Dennis Lo Breakthrough (Lancet 1997)

  • The Historical Dogma: Scientists spent decades unsuccessfully searching for intact fetal nucleated cells in maternal blood, which occur at negligible rates (1 in $10^7$ maternal cells).
  • The Liquid Biopsy Insight: In 1997, Dennis Lo hypothesized that fetal DNA exists outside cells as naked cell-free DNA (cfDNA) fragments in maternal blood plasma.
  • Y-Chromosome Proof: Lo detected male-specific Y-chromosome gene sequences (SRY) in the plasma of pregnant women carrying male fetuses, establishing that fetal DNA crosses the placental barrier.
Biological Discovery: Cell-free DNA constitutes ~3–10% of total circulating DNA in maternal plasma during early pregnancy, rising steadily until delivery.
Scientific Breakthrough: Fetal DNA circulates abundantly as cell-free fragments in maternal plasma rather than intact cells, enabling non-invasive liquid biopsy. LIQUID BIOPSY →
Movement III • Molecular Biology

cffDNA Biology: Placental Origin & Fragment Size

Why circulating 'fetal' DNA is biologically placental and shorter than maternal fragments.

Placental Biology & Fragment Physics

  • Placental Trophoblast Origin: Cell-free 'fetal' DNA originates from the placenta, not the fetus itself. Dying placental surface cells (syncytiotrophoblasts) shed DNA fragments directly into maternal blood.
  • Shorter Fragment Lengths (143 bp vs. 166 bp): Placental fragments are noticeably shorter (~143 base pairs) than maternal fragments (~166 base pairs). Maternal DNA stays longer because spool proteins (histones) protect connecting linker DNA.
  • Rapid Clearance Half-Life: Placental DNA has a rapid half-life (clearance time) of only 16 minutes. Maternal kidneys and liver completely eliminate all fragments within a few hours of delivery.
Biological Consequence: Because cffDNA clears within hours post-delivery, cell-free DNA from an earlier pregnancy never lingers to contaminate subsequent tests.
Biological Reality: Circulating 'fetal' DNA is derived from apoptotic placental trophoblasts, is shorter than maternal fragments (143 bp vs 166 bp), and clears within hours of birth. TROPHOBLAST DYNAMICS →
Movement III • Quality Metric

The Fetal Fraction Metric: Analytical Thresholds

Why test results are invalid when placental DNA drops below four percent.

Defining the Fetal Fraction Parameter

  • What is Fetal Fraction (FF)?: Fetal fraction (FF) is the proportion of total cell-free DNA in maternal plasma that originates from the placenta: $\text{FF} = \text{cffDNA} / \text{Total cfDNA}$ (typically 4% to 20%).
  • The 4% Clinical Floor: If fetal fraction is below 4%, the statistical signal of an extra fetal chromosome is indistinguishable from background sequencing noise, resulting in a test 'No-Call'.
  • Maternal BMI Dilution Effect: High maternal body weight significantly increases maternal adipose apoptosis, releasing vast amounts of maternal cfDNA that dilutes the fetal fraction.
Clinical Quality Control: A 'No-Call' result due to low fetal fraction carries an elevated risk of true fetal aneuploidy (particularly Trisomies 13 and 18, which cause small, stunted placentas).

Movement III • Sequencing Technology

Counting Chromosomes: Shotgun Sequencing & Z-Scores

How high-throughput sequencing detects minute statistical elevations in fetal chromosome counts.

Massively Parallel Shotgun Sequencing (MPSS)

  • Sequencing & Aligning Reads: High-throughput sequencers analyze 20 to 30 million cell-free fragments (reads) per patient. Software aligns (maps) each read to its matching chromosome.
  • The Normal Chromosome Baseline: Laboratories calculate what percentage of reads belongs to chromosome 21. In a normal (euploid, two-copy) pregnancy, chromosome 21 consistently represents ~1.30% of total reads.
  • Detecting Trisomy 21 via Z-Score: Trisomy 21 adds a third chromosome copy (+50% dosage). At 10% fetal DNA, Chr 21 reads rise by 5% ($50\% \times 10\%$, from 1.30% to 1.365%). A Z-score ≥ 3.0 (three standard deviations above normal) confirms this elevation.
Statistical Z-Score Formula: Z-score = (Observed % Chr 21 - Mean % in Controls) / (SD of Controls). A Z-score ≥ 3.0 indicates aneuploidy with 99.9% statistical confidence.
Statistical Signal: NIPT detects aneuploidy by sequencing millions of cell-free fragments and measuring minute percentage elevations (Z-scores ≥ 3.0) in chromosome read counts. Z-SCORE CALLING →
Movement III • Bayesian Statistics

Bayesian Principles: Sensitivity vs. Positive Predictive Value

Why a 99% sensitive test does NOT mean a 99% chance the fetus is affected.

Sensitivity vs. Positive Predictive Value

  • Analytical vs. Diagnostic Certainty: Commercial screening reports list '>99% Sensitivity', but sensitivity measures how well a test detects disease when present, not the probability that a positive result is true.
  • Prevalence Dictates Accuracy: Positive Predictive Value (PPV), the probability that a patient with a positive test truly has an affected fetus, is fundamentally driven by prior population disease prevalence.
  • False-Positive Discrepancy in Rare Cohorts: In a young patient cohort where Trisomy 21 prevalence is 1 in 1,000, a 1% false positive rate yields as many false positives as true positives, depressing PPV to 50%.
Bayesian Formula: PPV = True Positives / (True Positives + False Positives). Low disease prevalence mathematically depresses PPV.
Bayesian Reality: High analytical sensitivity does not guarantee clinical certainty: in low-prevalence cohorts, up to 50% of positive screening tests are false positives. PPV CALCULATION →

Movement III • Biological Confounders

Biological Confounders: Confined Placental Mosaicism

Why non-invasive prenatal screening can be discordantly abnormal while the fetus is healthy.

The Trophoblast Disconnect

  • Placental vs. Fetal Lineage: During early embryogenesis, the blastocyst separates into the inner cell mass (fetus) and trophectoderm (placenta).
  • Confined Placental Mosaicism (CPM): In 1–2% of pregnancies, a mitotic nondisjunction error occurs exclusively in the placenta, causing a trisomic placenta while the fetus remains completely euploid (normal).
  • Maternal Genomic Confounders: Maternal copy number variants, vanishing twin remnants, or undiagnosed maternal fibroids/malignancies shed abnormal cfDNA that mimics fetal aneuploidy.
Biological Limitation: Because NIPT sequences placental trophoblast DNA, a positive test cannot distinguish confined placental mosaicism from true fetal aneuploidy.
Biological Confounder: Confined placental mosaicism produces false-positive NIPT screens because circulating cell-free DNA reflects placental trophoblast biology rather than the fetus. CONFINED MOSAICISM →
Movement III • Clinical Governance

Clinical Governance: Screening vs. Diagnostic Testing

Professional medical guidelines strictly forbid irreversible action without invasive confirmation.

The ACMG & ACOG Clinical Mandate

  • Universal Clinical Consensus: The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and ACMG explicitly mandate that NIPT is a screening test, NEVER a diagnostic test.
  • Mandatory Invasive Confirmation: A positive NIPT screen must ALWAYS be confirmed by invasive diagnostic testing (amniocentesis or CVS) before irreversible decisions such as pregnancy termination are made.
  • Pre-Test and Post-Test Counseling: Patients must be counseled before testing regarding the possibility of false positives, false negatives, 'No-Call' results, and the necessity of diagnostic verification.
Medical-Legal Standard: Terminating a pregnancy based solely on an unconfirmed NIPT screen violates professional standards of care due to the documented occurrence of false positives from CPM.
Governance Mandate: NIPT is strictly a screening test; professional medical standards require invasive diagnostic testing to confirm any positive screen before taking irreversible action. CLINICAL MANDATE →

Lecture 4 • Course Synthesis

Synthesis: Structural Variation & Gene Dosage

The unified diagnostic framework from chromosomal rearrangements to liquid biopsy.

Core Conceptual Pillars of Lecture 4

  • Movement I • Gene Dosage Mechanics: Structural rearrangements alter gene copy number and cellular stoichiometry; triplosensitivity (CMT1A, HER2) and haploinsufficiency (HNPP) cause severe disease through wild-type protein imbalance.
  • Movement II • Cytogenetic Resolution: Diagnostic yield increases 100-fold from karyotypes (5–10 Mb) to microarrays (10–20 kb); SNP arrays simultaneously capture copy number shifts and copy-neutral loss of heterozygosity (UPD).
  • Movement III • Prenatal Liquid Biopsy: Non-invasive cell-free DNA screening transformed prenatal screening, but Bayesian principles mandate invasive diagnostic verification due to prevalence shifts and placental mosaicism.
The Master Diagnostic Rule: Always match the diagnostic test to the physical scale of the lesion, and interpret positive screening tests through the lens of prior disease prevalence.
