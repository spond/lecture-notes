Light Switches vs. Soundboards

In Lecture 2, a single base change in HBB caused sickle cell anemia. Why can't we find a "gene for extroversion" or a "gene for depression"?
Lecture 02 Paradigm 💡

The Monogenic "Light Switch"

A single high-impact mutation in one coding gene drives disease pathology with near-deterministic certainty.

  • • Genetic Target: 1 single causal gene (e.g. HBB, CFTR)
  • • Effect Size: Massive (Odds Ratio > 100)
  • • Mechanism: Direct physical disruption of 1 protein
  • • Cohort Needed: A few affected families
Example: Sickle Cell Anemia • Cystic Fibrosis • Huntington's
Lecture 03 Paradigm 🎛️

The Polygenic "Soundboard"

Thousands of independent DNA positions across all 22 autosomes, each nudging the phenotype by a fraction of a millimeter.

  • • Genetic Target: Thousands of distributed loci across autosomes
  • • Effect Size: Infinitesimal (β ≈ 0.01 SD)
  • • Mechanism: Omnigenic networks & chromatin regulation
  • • Cohort Needed: Hundreds of thousands to millions
Example: Extraversion • Height • Type 2 Diabetes • Depression
🧠 The Core Takeaway: Complex human temperaments are not governed by "broken master switches." They are the cumulative product of thousands of tiny genetic nudges interacting with an entire lifetime of environmental experience.

GWAS First Principles • Concept 2 of 7

The Genetic Bookmark: What is a SNP?

Before testing 1.14 million people, what does a single-letter DNA difference look like in the human genome?
Biological Foundations 🧬

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism

Humans share >99.9% of their 3.2 billion base pairs. Common heritable variation consists almost entirely of single-base substitutions ($A, C, G, T$).

  • • Frequency: ∼4–5 million common SNPs per individual (∼1 every 1,000 base pairs).
  • • Major vs. Minor Allele: Common ancestral base vs. less frequent derived variant in the population.
  • • Biallelic: Virtually all human SNPs have only two observed alleles (e.g. either $A$ or $G$).
Cataloged by: International HapMap Project • 1000 Genomes Consortium
Etymology & Nomenclature 📜

Origin of "SNP" vs. "SNV"

Coined in the late 1980s (pronounced "snip") as genetics shifted from restriction fragments (RFLPs) to single-base resolution.

The Classical 1% Threshold
Historically defined by Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) > 1%. Rarer variants were dismissed as "private mutations."
🔬 Modern Standard: SNV (Single Nucleotide Variant)
WGS reveals millions of rare & de novo variants; SNV is now the umbrella term for all single-base changes.
Rule of thumb: All SNPs are SNVs, but only common SNVs (>1%) are classical SNPs
🔖 The Core Takeaway: A SNP is not an exotic defect—it is the natural currency of human variation. In GWAS, common SNPs serve as permanent coordinate signposts across the genome.

GWAS First Principles • Concept 4 of 7

The Additive Dosage Model: 0, 1, 2

How GWAS translates discrete DNA letters into a linear numerical dose—and why each allele adds an identical nudge.
Mathematical Encoding 🔢

Counting the Effect Allele

Regression cannot compute letters like A/G. We designate one base as the Effect Allele (e.g., $G$) and count how many copies each participant carries:

  • x = 0 Non-carrier ($AA$): Baseline trait average ($\mu$).
  • x = 1 Heterozygote ($AG$): Shifts trait mean by $+\beta$.
  • x = 2 Homozygote ($GG$): Shifts trait mean by $+2\beta$.
🔬 What Schwaba et al. (2026) Did: Tested all 9.2 million SNPs under this exact additive linear model across 1.14 million participants ($y = \mu + \beta x_i + \epsilon$).
📊 The Core Takeaway: The additive model assumes alleles act like equal weights on a balance scale: two copies have exactly twice the effect of one ($2 \times \beta$). This simple linear assumption powers almost all modern GWAS discovery.
Additive Linearity
GWAS First Principles • Human Genetic Diversity

Variation in a Typical Human Genome

When we align any individual's DNA to the human reference (GRCh38), what differences do we actually find?
Reference Alignment Realities 🧬

1. ~4 to 5 Million Variant Sites (~0.1%)

Humans share 99.9% identical DNA across 3.1 billion base pairs. All human phenotypic diversity and complex trait variation arises from this ~0.1% fraction.

2. The Count vs. Size Paradox

SNPs dominate numerical count (>90% of variant sites, ~4.5M). Yet Structural Variants alter 5× more total sequence (~25–30 Mb) because single events delete or duplicate entire genomic blocks.

3. >98% Resides in Non-Coding Space

Only ~20,000 variants sit in protein-coding exons (~100 loss-of-function). The overwhelming majority sit in introns, enhancers, and promoters—where >90% of GWAS hits reside!

🔬 1000 Genomes & T2T Baseline: An average diploid human carries 4.1M–4.5M SNPs, 550k–600k short indels, and 20k–25k structural variants.

What is an "Effect" and How is it Measured?

In GWAS, an "effect" is not a master switch—it is a tiny nudge. How comparing average trait scores across millions of people reveals the additive power of allele clusters.
Core GWAS Intuition ⚖️

1. What is an "Effect" (β)?

An effect size (β) is simply a nudge. It answers: "On average, if a person carries one extra copy of this allele, how much does their trait score shift?"

2. How is it Measured?

By comparing the average trait scores of people with 0, 1, or 2 copies across the entire sample. Because survey scores are converted to a standard bell curve (Mean = 0, SD = 1), β is measured in fractions of a standard deviation (σ).

3. The Power of Allele Clusters:

In Schwaba et al., individual SNP effects are microscopic (β ≈ 0.01–0.03σ). A single SNP is completely imperceptible. But when you inherit a cluster of dozens of nudge alleles, their effects add together!

💡 Key Formula Insight: In $y_i = \mu + \beta x_i + \epsilon$, each allele copy adds $+\beta\sigma$ ($+0.02\sigma$). Two copies add $+2\beta\sigma$. Fifty copies add $+50\beta\sigma$ ($+1.00\sigma$)!

GWAS First Principles • Concept 7 of 7

Finding "Lead SNPs": Linear Regression Across the Genome

For every single SNP in the genome, we fit a linear regression across millions of participants. The slope (β) reveals whether the allele nudges the trait up, down, or not at all.
The Statistical Workflow 🔍

1. Fit Regression for Each SNP ($j$)

Plot each person's allele dosage ($x_i \in \{0, 1, 2\}$) against their standardized trait score ($y_i$). Fit the model:

$y_i = \mu + \beta_j x_{ij} + \gamma'\mathbf{C}_i + \epsilon_i$
$x_{ij}$: allele dosage • $\beta_j$: effect slope • $\mathbf{C}_i$: age, sex, ancestry PCs

2. Test the Null Hypothesis ($H_0: \beta_j = 0$)

Is the slope statistically different from a flat line ($\beta = 0$), or just noise? Because we run this test ~10 million times, a SNP must pass the genome-wide threshold: $p < 5 \times 10^{-8}$.

3. Identifying the "Lead SNP":

Nearby SNPs travel together in blocks (linkage disequilibrium), creating a cluster of significant signals. The variant with the strongest statistical association (lowest $p$-value) in that region is crowned the Lead SNP.

📊 Schwaba et al. (2026) Scale: Tested millions of variants across 1,141,408 people, identifying 1,260 independent lead loci across the Big Five personality traits.

GWAS First Principles • The Statistical Hurdle

The Multiple Testing Trap: Why $p < 0.05$ Fails in GWAS

A 1-in-20 coincidence is surprising once. Repeated a million times, it becomes a mathematical certainty. Why testing 10 million SNPs demands a much stricter threshold.
First Principles of Multiple Testing 🎲

1. The 20-Sided Die Analogy

Rolling a 20 on a fair 20-sided die has a probability of exactly $1/20 = 0.05$ (5%). In a standard lab experiment testing 1 gene, a $p < 0.05$ result is rare enough to treat as a potential discovery.

2. The 10-Million Test Disaster

If you roll the die 100 times, you expect five 20s. In a GWAS testing 10,000,000 SNPs, testing at $p < 0.05$ yields:

10,000,000 × 0.05 = 500,000 False Positives!
3. The Bonferroni Correction ($p < 5 \times 10^{-8}$):

To guarantee fewer than 5% false discoveries across the entire genome, divide $\alpha$ by the ~1,000,000 independent genomic blocks: $\alpha_{\text{GWAS}} = \frac{0.05}{1,000,000} = \mathbf{5 \times 10^{-8}}$

🛡️ The Golden Standard: $p < 5 \times 10^{-8}$ is the universal threshold in modern human genomics. Every one of the 1,260 lead SNPs in Schwaba et al. had to beat this ultra-strict cutoff!

GWAS First Principles • The Panoramic Output

The Manhattan Plot: The Skyline of the Genome

Arranging millions of single-SNP regressions across all 22 chromosomes to reveal towering skyscrapers of genetic discovery.
Anatomy of a Manhattan Plot 🏙️

1. Chromosomal Address ($X$-Axis)

Autosomes 1 through 22 are laid end-to-end. Every dot is a single SNP's regression test from Slide 9, positioned at its exact physical base-pair coordinate.

2. Statistical Tallness ($Y = -\log_{10} p$)

Tiny $p$-values are inverted so stronger statistical evidence reaches higher into the sky: $p = 0.01 \to \mathbf{2}$, while $p = 10^{-25} \to \mathbf{25}$! The taller the tower, the stronger the confidence.

3. The Genome-Wide Line ($5 \times 10^{-8}$)

The red dashed line ($-\log_{10} p = 7.3$) is the Bonferroni firewall from Slide 10. Anything below is background chance; anything piercing above is an official discovery.

4. Why "Skyscrapers"? (Linkage Disequilibrium):

Neighboring SNPs are physically chained on chromosomes and travel together in Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) blocks. When a causal variant exists, dozens of neighbors get dragged upward in tandem, forming a classic skyscraper!

📊 Schwaba et al. (2026): Tested ~10 million variants in 1.14M people; 1,260 towers pierced the $5 \times 10^{-8}$ line across the Big Five (824 novel loci!).

GWAS First Principles • Genomic Technology

Reading the Genome: SNP Arrays vs. Sequencing

Why mega-GWAS biobanks choose $50 "spot checks" over $500 whole-genome sequencing to assay millions of people.
Technology Comparison 🔬

1. SNP Microarrays: The "Spot Check" (~$50)

Glass chips with ~500,000 pre-printed microscopic DNA probes. Patient DNA binds complementary probes; a laser reads fluorescent allele tags.

2. Next-Gen Sequencing: The "Full Read" (~$500)

Reads all 3.2 billion letters base-by-base ($A \to C \to G \to T$). Discovers brand-new, ultra-rare mutations, but produces massive 100 GB files per person.

3. The Array Blind Spot

Microarrays are completely blind to novel variants: if a site has no pre-manufactured probe on the chip, it cannot be detected.

💰 The 1.14 Million Participant Math:

$50 Array × 1,141,408 = ~$57 Million (Feasible via consortium).
$500 Sequencing × 1,141,408 = ~$570 Million (Budget impossibility).

🔬 Schwaba et al. Choice: Assayed cohorts on affordable SNP chips, then leveraged population genetics to fill the un-probed gaps!

GWAS First Principles • The Genomic Architecture

Linkage Disequilibrium: The Hitchhikers of the Genome

Why DNA is inherited in blocks—and how measuring 500,000 "Tag SNPs" captures 10 million variants across the genome.
How Linkage Disequilibrium Works 🔗

1. DNA Travels in Blocks (Haplotypes)

Chromosomes swap chunks at recombination hotspots. Stretches of 10–100 kb are inherited intact as single units.

2. The "Hitchhiker" Effect ($r^2 \to 1.0$)

A causal mutation drags all neutral neighbors on its block along with it, creating the towering skyscrapers on Manhattan plots.

3. Tag SNPs & Imputation

Testing one Tag SNP per block on a $50 chip proxies millions of unsequenced variants via imputation.

⚠️ The Fine-Mapping Dilemma:

A GWAS lead SNP marks the neighborhood, not necessarily the causal driver trapped inside the block.

🔬 Schwaba et al. (2026): Assayed ~500k chip SNPs, then imputed ~10 million variants across 1.14M participants.

Genotype Imputation: The Genetic Autocomplete

How reference libraries allow a $50 chip measuring 500,000 markers to accurately predict 10 million variants.
How Imputation Works ⚡

1. The Sequencing Cost Wall

Sequencing 1.14M whole genomes would cost ~$500 million. Instead, $50 microarray chips read ~500,000 Tag SNPs (~5% of common sites).

2. The Reference Library (Haplotype Templates)

Consortia (1000 Genomes, HRC) fully sequenced thousands of diverse genomes, building an atlas of which alleles travel together in LD blocks.

3. Algorithmic "Autocomplete" ($r^2 \ge 0.8$)

Software matches the participant's chip tags to the library template, filling in millions of unsequenced letters with >95% accuracy.

✅ Strict Quality Control (INFO Metric)

Every imputed variant receives an INFO score ($0$ to $1$). Low-confidence predictions ($INFO < 0.8$) are strictly discarded before analysis.

🔬 Schwaba et al. (2026): Assayed ~500k chip markers, then accurately imputed 10,143,288 high-quality variants across all 1,141,408 participants!

Sample Size: Why We Needed 1.14 Million People

When each genetic nudge explains only 0.01% of variation, finding them requires massive cohort power.
The Mathematics of Power 📈

1. The Tiny Effect Reality ($\beta \approx 0.02\sigma$)

Polygenic traits are shaped by thousands of variants, each explaining just 0.01% of variance. There are no "master personality genes."

2. The $5 \times 10^{-8}$ Finish Line

To survive 1 million tests, true hits must clear $p < 5 \times 10^{-8}$. In a small cohort, tiny signals are completely buried by random sampling noise.

3. The Power Inflection ($\text{Power} \propto N \cdot \beta^2$)

Statistical power stays near zero until sample size crosses hundreds of thousands—then discovery suddenly explodes!

⚠️ Why 1990s "Candidate Gene" Studies Failed:

Small studies ($N \approx 300$) claimed "depression genes" with $p < 0.05$. With real effect sizes of $\beta = 0.02$, their power was 0.0%—yielding 100% false discoveries!

🔬 Schwaba et al. (2026): Combining 46 cohorts into 1,141,408 people pushed power above 95%, discovering 1,260 independent loci.

GWAS First Principles • Polygenic Prediction

The Polygenic Score (PRS): Summing the Nudges

How we compress millions of tiny genetic variants into a single score—and what that number actually tells us.
The Mathematics of Polygenic Scores 📊

1. The Weighted Sum: $\text{PRS}_i = \sum \hat{\beta}_j \cdot x_{ij}$

Each person's score is calculated by multiplying their allele count ($x_{ij} \in \{0, 1, 2\}$) by the GWAS effect size ($\hat{\beta}_j$) and summing across all SNPs.

2. The Population Bell Curve

By the Central Limit Theorem, summing thousands of small independent nudges forms a smooth normal distribution. Most people sit in the middle.

3. The $R^2 \approx 2\text{--}3\%$ Reality Check

A personality PRS explains only 2% to 3% of variance. It shifts population risk averages, but cannot predict individual personality.

⚠️ The "Genetic Fortune Teller" Myth:

GWAS Retrospective • The 2000s Vision vs. Reality

The Promise of GWAS: What We Hoped For vs. What We Found

Following the Human Genome Project, GWAS promised to unlock the genetic blueprint of common human disease. Why did reality look so different?
The 2005 Genomic Dream ✨

1. "Find the 5–10 Major Disease Genes"

Researchers expected common diseases (type 2 diabetes, hypertension, depression) to be driven by a handful of high-impact common risk alleles.

2. Rapid Drug Discovery Targets

Identifying disease alleles would directly point to defective proteins, providing immediate pharmaceutical targets for blockbuster cures.

3. Personalized Risk Prediction at Birth

A simple DNA test at birth would reliably forecast lifetime risk for common chronic illnesses, enabling proactive lifestyle prevention.

🔮 Expectation: Mendelian simplicity scaled to the common population.
The Empirical Reality 📉

1. Infinitesimal Effects & Omnigenic Spread

Instead of 5 master genes, traits are governed by tens of thousands of SNPs, each explaining <0.01% of variance (Odds Ratios 1.02–1.10).

2. The 93% Non-Coding Desert

Over 93% of GWAS hits fall in non-coding intergenic and intronic regions. They do not alter amino acids; they tweak regulatory dials.

3. Modest Clinical Prediction (R² ~ 2–8%)

For most complex psychiatric and behavioral traits, polygenic scores explain <5% of variance, offering weak individual diagnostic power.

⚠️ The Verdict: GWAS did not fail biology—it revealed that biology is deeply omnigenic.
⚖️ The Core Realization: GWAS was not a failure of method, but a failure of reductionist assumptions. Yet in specific disease areas, GWAS yielded billion-dollar clinical breakthroughs. Let's examine what succeeded and what failed.

Klein et al. (Science 2005) • How a 96-patient GWAS cracked the leading cause of blindness in older adults.
The Landmark Finding 👁️

Complement Factor H (CFH Y402H)

In 2005, one of the earliest GWAS analyzed just 96 cases and 50 controls. Despite the tiny sample, it detected an immense signal on chromosome 1q31.

Before GWAS, AMD was assumed to be simple ocular aging. GWAS proved it is an uncontrolled innate autoimmune attack on retinal pigment epithelial cells by the complement system.

GWAS Success Story 2 • Cardiology & Drug Discovery

Success Story: Lipids, Coronary Disease & PCSK9

How genetic variation in LDL regulation led to one of the most successful classes of cardiovascular drugs in history.
Human Genetic Validation 🫀

Finding "Natural Knockouts" in Humans

GWAS for circulating LDL cholesterol and coronary artery disease (CAD) repeatedly flagged loci regulating hepatic LDL clearance, specifically PCSK9 and ANGPTL3.

LOSS-OF-FUNCTION MUTATIONS: -88% CAD RISK

Individuals carrying naturally occurring loss-of-function variants in PCSK9 exhibited lifelong low LDL-C (~15 mg/dL) with zero negative health consequences.

💡 Nature's Phase III Trial: Lifelong genetic inhibition proved the target was safe and effective.
Therapeutic Impact 💉

Monoclonal Antibodies & siRNA Therapies

Drug developers designed monoclonal antibodies to mimic the loss-of-function allele: evolocumab (Repatha) and alirocumab (Praluent), slashing LDL by >60%.

Inclisiran (RNA Interference)

Today, small interfering RNA (siRNA) therapies like inclisiran silence PCSK9 in the liver with just two injections per year, saving thousands of lives from myocardial infarction.

📈 Double Probability of Success: Drug targets with human genetic evidence are 2.6× more likely to pass FDA approval.
🎯 Why PCSK9 Succeeded: CAD has high polygenicity, but circulating lipid biomarkers (LDL-C) provided an intermediate biochemical phenotype with direct causal links to coronary plaque.

Crohn's disease, Ulcerative Colitis & Rheumatoid Arthritis • Mapping shared cytokine architecture.
Pathway Identification 🛡️

Beyond HLA: Discovering Autophagy & Cytokines

Early geneticists knew the HLA region drove immune risk. But GWAS across >200 IBD and RA loci uncovered entirely unsuspected biology: autophagy (ATG16L1) and interleukin signaling (IL23R, TYK2, JAK2).

CROSS-DISEASE IMMUNOLOGICAL OVERLAP

GWAS revealed that Crohn's, Psoriasis, Ankylosing Spondylitis, and Rheumatoid Arthritis share common pathway mutations, proving they are distinct clinical faces of the same cytokine circuits.

🧬 Biological Clarity: Transformed autoimmune disease classification from organ anatomy to molecular pathways.

IL-12/23 Blockers (Ustekinumab, Risankizumab)

Discovery of protective variants in IL23R directly spurred the development of IL-23 and IL-12 antagonists, producing transformative remission rates in Crohn's disease and psoriasis.

JAK / TYK2 Inhibitors

Targeting the downstream Janus kinases pinpointed by GWAS (e.g., deucravacitinib for TYK2, tofacitinib for JAK) replaced blunt steroids with oral targeted therapies.

🌟 Repurposing Power: Genetic pathway mapping allowed drugs approved for arthritis to instantly treat gut inflammation.

GWAS Struggle 1 • Psychiatric Genetics & Complexity

The Struggle: Major Depressive Disorder (MDD)

Why hundreds of loci across 1.3 million participants yielded almost zero new clinical medications.
The Statistical Impasse 🧠

Years of Zero Hits (N = 10,000 to 50,000)

While AMD was solved with 96 people, early depression GWAS with 20,000+ patients found literally zero genome-wide significant hits. Candidate gene claims (5-HTTLPR) completely failed replication.

N > 1.3 MILLION → HUNDREDS OF MINUSCULE LOCI

Only massive mega-consortia (PGC) eventually detected ~178 hits. But each single SNP alters depression risk by less than 1.03-fold (OR ≈ 1.02).

📉 Variance Explained: A polygenic score explains <2% of individual depression risk.
Why Depression Resisted GWAS 🔍

1. Extreme Phenotypic Heterogeneity

DSM depression requires 5 of 9 symptoms (insomnia or hypersomnia, weight gain or loss). Two patients can be diagnosed with zero overlapping symptoms!

2. Overwhelming Environmental Modulation

Poverty, childhood trauma, chronic stress, and isolation heavily drive depression onset, drowning out tiny individual genetic effect sizes.

3. No Biopsy, No Accessible Tissue

Unlike blood in leukemia or retina in AMD, living human brain synapses cannot be biopsied to test how GWAS non-coding variants alter wiring.

⚠️ Reality: Zero novel psychiatric medications in the clinic have originated from depression GWAS hits.
🎯 The Lesson of MDD: Enormous sample sizes cannot overcome diagnostic blurriness and massive environmental noise. Complex psychiatric states are distributed brain states, not monogenic lesions.
PHENOTYPIC BLUR
GWAS Struggle 2 • Aging & Complex Demographics

The Struggle: Human Longevity & Lifespan

Why living past 100 years has stubbornly resisted common variant discovery.
The Familial Paradox ⏳

Centenarians Cluster in Families, but...

Twin studies suggest human lifespan is 20–25% heritable, and centenarian siblings are 8–17× more likely to reach age 100. Yet 15 years of GWAS on exceptional longevity have identified almost nothing.

THE ONLY TWO CONSISTENT LOCI:
  • APOE (ε4 allele): A massive penalty allele (reduces lifespan via Alzheimer's and vascular disease).
  • FOXO3: Minor insulin-signaling survival effect.
📉 Beyond APOE: Virtually zero common "fountain-of-youth" variants exist.
Why Longevity Resists GWAS 🧬

1. Natural Selection Eliminates Post-Reproductive Traits

Evolution selects for survival until reproduction and child-rearing. There has never been evolutionary pressure to select common alleles specifically optimizing survival at age 95.

2. Longevity is the Absence of Hundreds of Lethal Risks

Reaching 100 is not a single biological pathway; it is surviving cancer, heart attacks, sepsis, diabetes, and stroke. It is the composite non-failure of every organ system.

3. Demographic Confounding & Cohort Shifts

People born in 1910 survived childhood infections, wars, and poor sanitation. Comparing them to controls born in 1980 introduces severe historical survival bias.

💡 The Mechanism: Extreme lifespan is driven by the *absence* of private deleterious mutations + lucky environmental accidents.
🎯 The Lesson of Longevity: Composite downstream phenotypes that depend on avoiding every possible cause of death cannot be captured by standard common-variant GWAS.
