Clinical Trials, Evidence-Based Medicine, and Drug Efficacy

Why 90% of drugs that cure preclinical mouse models fail in human clinical trials.
Slide 01
🎯 Biological Plausibility vs. Human Survival
  • Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM), the discipline that bases clinical decisions on controlled human trials rather than mechanistic intuition, protects patients from ineffective treatments.
  • Clinical Attrition Rate, the fraction of drugs entering human Phase I testing that never win FDA approval, reaches 90% overall and 92.1% in oncology.

Why Candidate Drugs Fail in Human Trials

Over 85% of clinical failures stem from lack of human efficacy or dose-limiting organ toxicity.
Slide 02
🧭 Three Root Causes of Human Trial Failure
  • Target Validation Failure (~55%): The drug engages its molecular target in patients, but blocking that target fails to slow human disease.
  • Human Organ Toxicity (~30%): Liver, heart, or bone marrow damage appears below the dose required for efficacy.
  • Genomic Rescue (2× Success): Targets backed by germline variants, inherited DNA changes present from birth, or GWAS, population-wide genetic scans, succeed twice as often ($16\%\text{ vs. }8\%$).

Historical Foundations • The Patent Medicine Era (Pre-1906)

Before Regulation: Morphine Syrups & Radioactive Water

What happened when anyone could bottle narcotics or radium and market them as cures without testing.
Slide 03
🧪 Secret Poisons Sold Over the Counter
  • Mrs. Winslow’s Soothing Syrup (1849–1930), an infant teething tonic, hid $65\text{ mg}$ of morphine per fluid ounce—killing thousands of babies via respiratory arrest, the fatal cessation of breathing.
  • Radithor (“Radioactive Water”, 1918–1932) laced water with Radium-226 and Radium-228; industrialist Eben Byers drank $\sim 1,400$ bottles and died of jaw necrosis, the death and decay of living bone tissue.
  • The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act forced manufacturers to disclose narcotics on the label, yet required zero animal or human safety trials before selling a drug!
Historical Lesson: Ingredient labels alone cannot protect patients when untested chemicals are sold directly to the public.

Historical Foundations • Birth of Pre-Market Safety (1937–1938)

The 1937 Elixir Sulfanilamide Disaster

How dissolving an antibiotic in industrial antifreeze killed 107 patients and created Phase I safety testing.
Slide 04
☠️ Testing for Flavor Instead of Toxicity
  • The Fatal Formulation (Sept 1937): To make a liquid antibiotic, S.E. Massengill chemists dissolved sulfanilamide in 72% diethylene glycol, an industrial antifreeze solvent, with zero animal testing.
  • 107 Kidney Failure Deaths: Over $100$ patients, mostly children, died of anuria, the complete shutdown of urine production, and renal necrosis, the death of kidney tissue.
  • The 1938 FD&C Act: Congress mandated pre-market toxicology, laboratory testing of whether a chemical poisons living organs—yet still did not require proof that a drug worked!
Regulatory Milestone: The 1937 antifreeze tragedy created the FDA pre-market safety mandate (Phase I), ending untested chemical formulations.

Historical Foundations • Mandating Controlled Efficacy (1961–1962)

Thalidomide (1961) & The Kefauver–Harris Mandate

How a sedative marketed on physician testimonials caused >10,000 birth defects and birthed modern Phase I–III RCTs.
Slide 05
🧬 Teratogenesis (Fetal Malformation) & Modern RCTs
  • Phocomelia, a severe congenital malformation where hands and feet attach directly to the trunk due to missing long limb bones, struck $>10,000$ infants whose mothers took Thalidomide.
  • Mechanism & Dr. Frances Kelsey: Thalidomide hijacks CRBN, a cellular protein-recycling enzyme, to destroy SALL4, a fetal DNA switch that directs limb outgrowth; FDA reviewer Frances Kelsey blocked U.S. approval for lacking controlled data.
  • The 1962 Kefauver–Harris Amendment: Congress mandated substantial evidence of efficacy in “adequate and well-controlled investigations”—establishing modern Phases I, II, and III!
Core Synthesis: Every pillar of modern FDA clinical trials—Phase I toxicology, informed consent, and Phase II/III RCTs—was forged by these three disasters.

Historical Foundations • Concurrent Control Groups

James Lind (1747): The First Concurrent Controlled Trial

Testing six scurvy remedies simultaneously under identical shipboard conditions.
Slide 06
🍋 Isolating the Treatment Variable Aboard HMS Salisbury
  • Concurrent Control Group, a comparison group observed at the same time and in the same environment as the treated group, prevents weather or diet changes from mimicking a cure.
  • Lind's 1747 Design: Lind placed 12 sailors with scurvy, a lethal connective-tissue breakdown caused by vitamin C deficiency, in the same bay on an identical diet and divided them into six pairs.
  • Decisive Result: Only the pair given two oranges and one lemon daily recovered within six days; vinegar, cider, sulfuric acid, and seawater failed.
Remaining Flaw: Lind did not assign sailors at random, leaving tiny non-random groups vulnerable to selection bias.

Historical Foundations • Birth of the Modern RCT

Austin Bradford Hill (1948): The First Randomized Trial

How concealed random envelopes and blinded radiologists proved streptomycin cures tuberculosis.
Slide 07
🎲 The 1948 MRC Streptomycin Tuberculosis Trial
  • Allocation Concealment, hiding the upcoming randomization sequence inside sealed numbered envelopes until after enrollment, prevents doctors from steering favored patients into the drug arm.
  • Blinded Outcome Assessment: Two independent radiologists scored chest X-rays without knowing which patients received streptomycin.
  • 6-Month Result ($N=107$): Streptomycin cut mortality from 27% down to 7% ($p < 0.001$) and improved chest X-rays in 51% vs. 8% of patients.
Modern Standard: Concealed randomization + blinded assessment remains the foundation of FDA Phase III approval today.
The 1948 MRC Streptomycin Trial: Birth of the Modern RCT p < 0.001 Survival Gain

Causal Inference & Randomization • Confounding

Why Observational Studies Mislead: Confounding

How the “Healthy-User Effect” made Hormone Replacement Therapy look protective against heart attacks.
Slide 08
⚠️ The Observational HRT Illusion ($N = 70,533$)
  • Confounding, a distortion where a hidden third factor ($Z$) independently drives both treatment choice ($X$) and clinical outcome ($Y$), creates fake drug benefits in observational cohorts.
  • Healthy-User Bias: In the Nurses' Health Study, women taking Hormone Replacement Therapy (HRT), post-menopause estrogen-progestin pills, appeared to have 44% fewer heart attacks ($\text{RR} = 0.56$).
  • The Hidden Cause: Women who requested HRT were wealthier, exercised more, and had better preventive cardiology care than non-users.
Statistical Trap: Enrolling more patients ($N = 70,000$) narrows the confidence interval around the wrong answer without removing confounding!
Confounding in Observational Cohorts: The HRT Illusion Backdoor Path Z

Causal Inference & Randomization • The WHI Reversal

How Randomization Severs Confounding: The 2002 WHI Trial

Flipping a coin across 16,608 women proved that HRT actually increased heart attacks by 29%.
Slide 09
✂️ Severing the Backdoor Path ($Z \not\to X$)
  • Why Randomization Works: Assigning treatment by a random coin flip guarantees that both known and unknown confounders are split $50:50$ between arms.
  • The 2002 WHI RCT ($N=16,608$): When the Women's Health Initiative randomized women to HRT versus placebo, HRT increased coronary heart disease by +29% ($\text{HR} = 1.29$).
  • Immediate Clinical Reversal: The trial was halted early after also revealing higher rates of stroke ($+41\%$) and invasive breast cancer ($+26\%$).
Causal Rule: Only randomization severs the link between patient lifestyle ($Z$) and treatment assignment ($X$).
The 2002 Women's Health Initiative (WHI) RCT Reversal HR = 1.29 Harm

Causal Inference & Randomization • Time-Zero Biases

Immortal Time Bias: How Misaligned Clocks Rig Survival

Why counting pre-treatment waiting time makes even a useless drug look like it doubles survival.
Slide 10
⏳ How Waiting for a Prescription Rigs the Survival Clock
  • Immortal Time Bias, a survival distortion where patients must survive a waiting period—such as 6 months after diagnosis for genetic testing—to receive a drug, falsely credits that pre-drug survival time to the treatment.
  • Trap 1 — Free “Immortal” Months: A patient who starts a pill at Month 6 could not possibly have died during Months 0–6; counting Months 0–6 as “treated survival” gifts the drug 6 guaranteed death-free months!
  • Trap 2 — Dumping Early Deaths into Control: Any patient who dies at Month 2 or 3 before reaching the Month 6 prescription window is automatically labeled “Untreated,” dragging down the control curve.
The RCT Fix (Synchronized Time Zero): Randomized trials start the survival clock at the exact moment of Randomization (Time Zero) for both arms simultaneously.
Immortal Time Bias: How Pre-Drug Waiting Time Rigs Survival Time-Zero Alignment

Anatomy of a Double-Blind RCT (CONSORT Standard)

Why modern trials mask three separate groups and test new drugs against the Standard of Care.
Slide 11
🛡️ Triple-Blinding & Active Control Ethics
  • Double- / Triple-Blind Masking conceals treatment identity from (1) the patient, (2) the treating physician, and (3) the central radiologist/statistician scoring scans.
  • Preventing Expectation Bias: Masking prevents patients on placebo from reporting worse symptoms and prevents doctors from ordering extra scans for one arm.
  • Standard of Care (SoC), the best proven existing therapy for a disease, is given to the control arm whenever withholding treatment would be unethical.
CONSORT Standard, the mandatory international checklist for reporting clinical trials, requires a flowchart accounting for every enrolled patient.

Clinical Trial Design • Analytical Integrity

Intention-to-Treat (ITT) vs. Per-Protocol Analysis

Why throwing out patients who stop taking their pills rigs a trial in favor of the drug.
Slide 12
⚖️ “Once Randomized, Always Analyzed”
  • Intention-to-Treat (ITT) Analysis keeps every randomized patient in their assigned group (Drug vs. Placebo) no matter what—even if they feel sick and stop taking the pills on Day 2.
  • Why Per-Protocol Analysis Rigs Trials: Counting only compliant pill-takers is biased because when a drug causes side effects, the sickest, frailest patients stop taking it—leaving only the healthiest survivors in the Drug arm!
  • The 1980 Sugar-Pill Proof: In the Clofibrate heart trial, patients who faithfully took $\ge 80\%$ of their placebo sugar pills had half the death rate of poor sugar-pill takers ($15.1\%\text{ vs. }28.2\%$)—known as the Healthy-Adherer Effect.
FDA Rule: Never delete patients who stop taking their pills; primary efficacy must always be tested in the full Intention-to-Treat (ITT) group.
Intention-to-Treat (ITT) vs. Per-Protocol Bias (NEJM 1980) ITT Mandate

Clinical Trial Phases • Regulatory Pipeline

The Four Clinical Trial Phases (Phases I–IV)

How sample size, study architecture, and primary endpoints evolve from first-in-human dosing to real-world surveillance.
Slide 13
🪜 Sequential Escalation of Human Evidence
  • Phase I — Safety & Dosing ($N=20\text{–}80$): Escalates doses in small cohorts to measure pharmacokinetics (PK), how the body absorbs and clears the drug, and find the Maximum Tolerated Dose (MTD), the highest dose without severe toxicity.
  • Phase II — Proof-of-Concept ($N=100\text{–}300$): Tests whether the drug shrinks tumors or improves biomarkers in patients with the target disease, validating the genomic target before a massive trial.
  • Phase III ($N=300\text{–}3,000+$) & Phase IV ($N=10,000+$): Phase III compares the drug against Standard of Care in a pivotal double-blind RCT to win FDA approval; Phase IV tracks pharmacovigilance, real-world monitoring for rare side effects after approval.
Why Phase IV Exists: A 1,000-patient Phase III trial is too small to detect a 1-in-5,000 fatal toxicity—requiring post-approval surveillance across tens of thousands of patients.
The Four Clinical Trial Phases (Phases I–IV) Phases I–IV

Clinical Trial Phases • Phase I Dose-Finding Math

Phase I Dose Escalation: The 3+3 Algorithm

How cohorts of three patients identify the Maximum Tolerated Dose (MTD) without exposing large groups to toxicity.
Slide 14
🧪 Rules of the 3+3 Escalation Design
  • Dose-Limiting Toxicity (DLT), a severe Grade $\ge 3$ organ toxicity occurring during Cycle 1 ($28\text{ days}$), governs whether the next cohort escalates.
  • The Three 3+3 Rules: If 0/3 patients have a DLT, escalate; if 1/3 has a DLT, expand by adding 3 more at that dose; if $\ge 2$ patients (2/3 or 2/6) have a DLT, stop!
  • Defining the MTD: When $\ge 2$ patients suffer a DLT, the previous lower dose ($d - 1$) is declared the Maximum Tolerated Dose (MTD).
Modern Update (FDA Project Optimus): For targeted drugs, pick the Optimal Biologic Dose (OBD), the lowest dose that fully blocks the target receptor without excess toxicity.
The Phase I (3+3) Dose-Escalation Decision Tree MTD = Dose (d − 1)
Ref: Le Tourneau et al. (JNCI 2009); FDA Project Optimus (2024) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS

Survival Analysis • Reading Time-to-Event Curves

Kaplan–Meier Curves (Part 1): Step Drops & Median Survival

How clinical trials plot survival probability $S(t)$ over time and identify the 50% survival milestone.
Slide 16
📉 Tracking Survival Probability Over Time
  • Kaplan–Meier Curve, a step-function plot estimating the fraction of patients surviving past time $t$, starts at $S(0) = 1.00$ ($100\%$) on Randomization Day.
  • Vertical Step Drops: The curve stays flat between events and drops vertically at the exact timestamp a patient dies or progresses.
  • Median Survival: Draw a horizontal line from 0.50 (50%) on the Y-axis to each curve and drop vertically to the X-axis ($24\text{ vs. }12\text{ Mo}$).
Why Medians Beat Means: We use Median survival because you don't have to wait 20 years for the last longest-living survivor to die!

Survival Analysis • Censoring & Tail Stability

Kaplan–Meier Curves (Part 2): Right Censoring & Number at Risk

What vertical tick marks (|) mean—and why the right tail becomes noisy when few patients remain.
Slide 17
📏 Vertical Tick Marks (|) Are Survivors, Not Deaths!
  • Right Censoring (|) occurs when a patient is still alive when the study closes or moves away before experiencing an event.
  • How Ticks Affect the Curve: At a censoring tick (|), the curve does not drop, but that patient is removed from the future Number-at-Risk denominator.
  • Right-Tail Instability: When the Number at Risk drops below 10 at late timepoints, a single death causes a massive vertical plunge!
Forensic Rule: Always read the Number-at-Risk table beneath the X-axis before trusting the far-right tail of a survival curve.

Hazard Ratios & Subgroup Analysis • Cox Proportional Hazards

The Hazard Ratio ($\text{HR}$): Comparing Whole Survival Curves

Why a Hazard Ratio summarizes the entire trial better than a single 50% Median Survival slice.
Slide 18
📐 Instantaneous Event Rate Ratio Across Time
  • Hazard Ratio ($\text{HR} = h_{\text{drug}}(t) / h_{\text{control}}(t)$) measures the ratio of instantaneous event rates between the treatment and control arms across the entire study.
  • Three Benchmark Values: $\text{HR} = 1.0$ means zero effect; $\text{HR} = 0.58$ means a 42% lower instantaneous death rate ($1 - 0.58$); $\text{HR} = 2.03$ means death rate doubled.
  • What $\text{HR} = 0.58$ Does NOT Mean: It does not mean 42% of patients are cured forever; it compares the rate of events per unit time.
Advantage Over Medians: Median survival only looks at one horizontal slice ($50\%$); the Hazard Ratio uses every event from Day 1 to study end.
Hazard Ratio (HR) Interpretation: Protective (<1), Null (1), Harmful (>1) HR = h1(t) / h0(t)
Ref: Spruance et al. (Antimicrob Agents Chemother 2004; 48:2787–2792) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS
Hazard Ratios & Subgroup Analysis • Forest Plots

Reading a Subgroup Forest Plot & 95% Confidence Intervals

How to tell in two seconds whether a genetic subgroup truly benefits from a drug.
Slide 19
🌲 Squares, Whiskers & The Vertical Null Line ($\text{HR} = 1.0$)
  • Forest Plot, a graphical summary displaying the Hazard Ratio and 95% Confidence Interval (95% CI) across patient subgroups, anchors on the vertical line of no effect at $\text{HR} = 1.0$.
  • The 95% CI Crossing Rule: If the horizontal 95% CI whisker touches or crosses $\text{HR} = 1.0$ ($\text{HR} = 0.70\text{ [0.36--1.24]}$), the subgroup effect is statistically non-significant ($p > 0.05$).
  • Square Size = Sample Size: Larger squares represent larger subgroups with more events and narrower confidence intervals.
Quick Rule: Entire whisker left of $1.0$ = significant benefit; whisker crosses $1.0$ = non-significant; entire whisker right of $1.0$ = significant harm.
Reading a Subgroup Forest Plot & 95% Confidence Intervals Crossing 1.0 = NS

Hazard Ratios & Subgroup Analysis • Non-Proportional Hazards

When Survival Curves Cross (Part 1): The IPASS Puzzle

Why an unscreened targeted drug trial lost to chemotherapy for 6 months—then beat chemotherapy from Months 6 to 24.
Slide 20
🔀 Why Did the Survival Curves Cross in NCT00322452?
  • Enrolling by Clinical Profile Instead of DNA ($N=1,217$): In the 2009 IPASS trial, doctors enrolled Asian never-smokers with lung adenocarcinoma, a cancer of mucus-secreting lung cells, and randomized them to the EGFR blocker Gefitinib vs. chemotherapy—assuming clinical traits alone predicted who had EGFR-driven tumors.
  • Why Gefitinib Crashed Early (Months 0–6): Roughly $40\%$ of those never-smokers actually lacked an EGFR mutation; for them, Gefitinib was an inactive pill, so their tumors grew immediately while chemotherapy worked.
  • Why Gefitinib Won Late (Months 6–24): Once the $40\%$ non-responders progressed early, the remaining $60\%$ with mutant EGFR stayed in long-term remission on Gefitinib after chemotherapy wore off—causing the curves to cross at Month 6!
Proportional Hazards Violation: When survival curves cross, a single Hazard Ratio ($\text{HR} = 0.74$) is invalid because it averages early harm in one subgroup with late benefit in another!
The IPASS Puzzle: Crossing Curves in Unscreened Lung Cancer Proportional Hazards Violated
Ref: Mok et al. / IPASS Trial (NEJM 2009; 361:947–957, NCT00322452) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS

When Curves Cross (Part 2): Genomic Resolution by EGFR

Clinical Risk Math • Relative vs. Absolute Benefit

Relative Risk Reduction ($\text{RRR}$) vs. Absolute Risk Reduction ($\text{ARR}$)

Why a drug ad claiming “Cuts risk by 50%!” can mean saving 200 lives per 1,000—or saving just 1 life per 1,000.
Slide 22
📊 Separating Marketing Headlines from Real Lives Saved
  • Control Event Rate ($\text{CER}$) vs. Experimental Event Rate ($\text{EER}$): Compare the fraction of placebo patients who suffer a bad event ($\text{CER}$) against the fraction who suffer that event on the drug ($\text{EER}$).
  • Relative Risk Reduction ($\text{RRR} = \frac{\text{CER} - \text{EER}}{\text{CER}}$) — The Proportional Drop: Measures what fraction of baseline risk disappears; cutting deaths in half from $40\% \to 20\%$ ($400 \to 200\text{ per 1,000}$) and from $0.2\% \to 0.1\%$ ($2 \to 1\text{ per 1,000}$) both equal a 50% $\text{RRR}$ because $\text{RRR}$ ignores how rare the event was to start with!
  • Absolute Risk Reduction ($\text{ARR} = \text{CER} - \text{EER}$) — True Percentage-Point Gain: Subtracting the two rates reveals real patient impact: $40\% - 20\% = \mathbf{20.0\%}$ (200 lives saved per 1,000) versus $0.2\% - 0.1\% = \mathbf{0.1\%}$ (only 1 life saved per 1,000, while 999 take the pill for zero benefit).
Communication Rule: Drug advertisements quote $\text{RRR}$ to make tiny gains look huge; clinicians calculate $\text{ARR}$ to tell patients their true chance of benefiting.
Relative Risk Reduction (RRR) vs. Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) ARR = CER − EER
Ref: Nuovo et al. (JAMA 2002; 287:2813–2814) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS
Clinical Risk Math • NNT & Benefit-Risk Calibration

Number Needed to Treat ($\text{NNT}$) vs. Harm ($\text{NNH}$)

How genomic biomarkers collapse the Number Needed to Treat from 1,000 down to 5.
Slide 23
🧮 $\text{NNT} = 1 / \text{ARR}$: How Many Patients Must Take the Drug to Save 1 Life?
  • Number Needed to Treat ($\text{NNT} = \frac{1}{\text{ARR}}$) is the number of patients who must receive the drug to prevent one additional bad outcome.
  • Biomarker Enrichment Effect: When $\text{ARR} = 0.20$ ($40\% \to 20\%$), $\text{NNT} = 1 / 0.20 = \mathbf{5}$; when $\text{ARR} = 0.001$ ($0.2\% \to 0.1\%$), $\text{NNT} = \mathbf{1,000}$.
  • Number Needed to Harm ($\text{NNH} = \frac{1}{\text{ARI}}$): A drug with a 2% severe toxicity rate ($\text{NNH} = 50$) is a triumph when $\text{NNT} = 5$, but unacceptable when $\text{NNT} = 1,000$.
Clinical Approval Threshold: Precision biomarkers ensure that $\text{NNT} \ll \text{NNH}$ so net benefit outweighs drug toxicity.
Number Needed to Treat (NNT) vs. Number Needed to Harm (NNH) NNT = 1 / ARR
Ref: Laupacis, Sackett & Roberts (NEJM 1988; 318:1728–1733) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS
Trial Power & Interim Monitoring • DSMB Governance

Statistical Power ($1-\beta$) & DSMB Stopping Rules

How independent Data and Safety Monitoring Boards stop trials early for overwhelming efficacy or harm.
Slide 24
🛑 Type I ($\alpha$) Error, Power ($1-\beta$) & Alpha-Spending
  • Type I Error ($\alpha = 0.05$) vs. Power ($1-\beta = 90\%$): $\alpha$ caps false positives at 5%, while Statistical Power is the probability of detecting a true drug benefit—driven by total events rather than enrolled headcount.
  • Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), an independent panel of outside experts with unblinded access to interim data, conducts pre-planned safety and efficacy checks.
  • O'Brien–Fleming Stopping Rule: Stopping a trial at the first interim look requires an ultra-strict $p < 0.0005$ so repeated peeking does not inflate false positives.
Ethical Balance: The DSMB stops a trial early if the drug is so effective that placebo is unethical—or if the drug is causing harm.
Statistical Power (1 − β) & DSMB Interim Stopping Rules Alpha-Spending Rule

Clinical vs. Surrogate Endpoints • RECIST 1.1

Primary Clinical Endpoints (OS) vs. Surrogates (PFS & ORR)

Why measuring tumor shrinkage on a CT scan is faster than waiting for mortality—and why the two diverge.
Slide 25
🎯 How a Patient Feels, Functions, or Survives
  • Overall Survival (OS), the time from randomization until death from any cause, is the gold-standard primary endpoint because it integrates both drug efficacy and fatal drug toxicity.
  • Surrogate Endpoint, a scan or lab marker that substitutes for survival, includes Objective Response Rate (ORR), the fraction of patients with $\ge 30\%$ tumor shrinkage, and Progression-Free Survival (PFS), the time until a tumor grows or the patient dies.
  • RECIST 1.1, the standardized radiology ruler for scoring CT/MRI scans, defines a Partial Response as $\ge 30\%$ diameter shrinkage and Progressive Disease (PD) as $\ge 20\%$ growth.
Endpoint Hierarchy: ORR proves biological tumor shrinkage; PFS measures disease control duration; only OS proves net survival gain.
RECIST 1.1 Tumor Cutoffs & Primary vs. Surrogate Endpoints ORR, PFS & OS

Clinical vs. Surrogate Endpoints • The Surrogate Paradox

The Surrogate Paradox (Part 1): The CAST Trial Disaster

How encainide and flecainide suppressed premature heartbeats by 80% while doubling sudden cardiac death.
Slide 26
⚠️ Winning the Biomarker While Losing the Patient
  • The Surrogate Paradox occurs when an experimental drug significantly improves a surrogate biomarker yet increases overall patient mortality.
  • The 1989 CAST Trial ($N=1,498$): After heart attacks, encainide and flecainide suppressed premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), extra skipped heartbeats in the lower chambers, by $>80\%$.
  • Fatal Reversal: Instead of saving lives, the drugs more than doubled mortality ($8.3\%\text{ vs. }3.5\%, \text{RR} = 2.5$) by triggering ventricular arrhythmias, chaotic rhythms that stop the heart from pumping!
Historical Lesson: Normalizing a laboratory or ECG number never guarantees that a patient will live longer.
The Surrogate Paradox: CAST Antiarrhythmic Trial (NEJM 1989) RR = 2.5 Mortality

Clinical vs. Surrogate Endpoints • Oncology Surrogate Failure

The Surrogate Paradox (Part 2): The BELLINI Myeloma Trial

How Venetoclax doubled Progression-Free Survival (HR = 0.63) while doubling patient deaths (OS HR = 2.03).
Slide 27
🔬 Dissecting NCT02755597 (Lancet Oncology 2020)
  • What a BCL-2 Blocker Does: BCL-2 is an anti-apoptotic survival protein that shields cells from apoptosis, programmed cell self-destruction. Venetoclax blocks BCL-2, stripping away this survival shield so BCL-2-dependent cells immediately self-destruct.
  • Surrogate Victory vs. Survival Harm: In multiple myeloma, a cancer of bone-marrow plasma cells, Venetoclax nearly doubled median PFS ($\text{HR} = 0.63$), yet doubled Overall Survival mortality ($\text{OS HR} = 2.03$)!
  • Why Non-t(11;14) Patients Died: Only tumors with the t(11;14) translocation, a chromosome 11-to-14 swap that drives BCL-2, shrank ($\text{PFS HR} = 0.11, \text{OS HR} = 0.61$). Non-t(11;14) tumors use a different shield (MCL-1), so Venetoclax spared the tumor while wiping out healthy immune cells, causing fatal sepsis, bloodstream infection.
Precision Rule: If the FDA had approved Venetoclax for all myeloma patients based on PFS alone, it would have killed non-t(11;14) patients!
BELLINI Trial (NCT02755597): Unstratified Harm vs. t(11;14) Rescue t(11;14) Biomarker
Ref: Kumar et al. / BELLINI (Lancet Oncol 2020; 21:1630–1642, NCT02755597) BIOL 3111 • CLINICAL TRIALS

FDA Accelerated Approval: The 2019 HOPE Voxelotor Trial

Granting early access in Sickle Cell Disease based on a +1.1 g/dL hemoglobin rise (NCT03036813).
Slide 28
🏛️ Provisional Approval on an Unvalidated Lab Surrogate
  • FDA Accelerated Approval (Subpart H) grants provisional market access based on a surrogate endpoint “reasonably likely to predict clinical benefit,” contingent on confirmatory trials.
  • The 2019 HOPE Trial (NCT03036813): Voxelotor (Oxbryta) raised total hemoglobin by $>1.0\text{ g/dL}$ in 51% vs. 7% of Sickle Cell patients at Week 24 ($p < 0.001$).

The Sept 2024 Voxelotor Recall: The Oxygen Affinity Trap

Why locking sickle hemoglobin in the oxygenated state starved tissues of $\text{O}_2$ and increased patient deaths.
Slide 29
🩸 High Hemoglobin That Refuses to Release Oxygen
  • The Biophysical Flaw: Voxelotor locks $\sim 30\%$ of sickle hemoglobin (HbS) in the relaxed (R) state, the structural conformation that binds oxygen tightly and resists sickling.
  • Starving Tissues: Because R-state hemoglobin refuses to release $\text{O}_2$ in hypoxic capillaries, oxygen-starved micro-vessels, blood viscosity rose while tissue oxygen delivery fell!
  • Sept 25, 2024 Global Recall: Post-marketing confirmatory trials revealed higher vaso-occlusive crisis rates and excess deaths in the Voxelotor arms, forcing immediate global withdrawal.
Biophysical Lesson: A chemically manipulated lab surrogate (+1.1 g/dL Hb) can mask functional tissue hypoxia—proving why confirmatory clinical outcome trials are mandatory.

When a Biomarker Leads Recovery: The VALOR ALS Trial

How a 60% drop in blood nerve-damage protein (NfL) predicted 1-year muscle preservation in Lou Gehrig's disease.
Slide 30
🧠 Stopping Nerve Death Before Muscle Strength Stabilizes
  • Disease Context (ALS / Lou Gehrig's Disease): Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal paralysis in which the motor neurons controlling movement and breathing progressively die. In inherited SOD1-ALS, a mutated SOD1 protein poisons these nerves.
  • Measuring Nerve Death in Blood (NfL): When motor neurons die, their internal structural protein—Neurofilament Light Chain (NfL)—leaks into the blood. A drop in blood NfL directly indicates that fewer nerve cells are dying.
  • Why Blood NfL Led Muscle Recovery (VALOR Trial): The gene-silencing drug Tofersen shut down toxic SOD1 and cut blood NfL by 60% by Week 16. Because spared nerves take months to stabilize muscle function, physical strength scores only separated at Week 52 ($p = 0.027$), earning FDA approval.
Clinical Lesson: In slow neurodegenerative diseases, blood NfL detects that nerve cells have stopped dying months before physical strength tests can measure the difference.

Basket Trials: One Mutation Across Many Tumor Organs

How Larotrectinib (NCT02122913) won tissue-agnostic FDA approval across 17 NTRK-fusion cancer types.
Slide 32
🧺 Solving the Rarity Problem in Precision Oncology
  • Basket Trial, a histology-agnostic (tissue-agnostic) design that enrolls patients across many different tumor organs who share one genomic mutation, treats all patients with one matched drug.
  • Why Basket Trials Are Needed: NTRK1/2/3 gene fusions, hybrid genes formed when broken chromosomes weld together, occur in $<0.3\%$ of common cancers, making single-organ trials impossible.
  • Landmark Proof (NCT02122913, NEJM 2018): Pooling 55 NTRK-fusion patients across 17 organ types into one basket proved a 75% Objective Response Rate for Larotrectinib!
Memory Rule: Basket Trial = 1 shared mutation + 1 drug pooling patients from many different organs into one basket.

Umbrella Trials & Adaptive Platform Trials

Routing one cancer organ into parallel mutation arms (Lung-MAP) and sharing a dynamic control arm (I-SPY 2).
Slide 33
☂️ Umbrella Sub-Arms & How Adaptive Control Arms Work
  • Umbrella Trial (Lung-MAP): Screens patients with one cancer type (lung cancer) and routes each mutation (EGFR, KRAS G12C, MET, RET) to its own matched drug arm—assigning nearly every screened patient to an active study.
  • How the Shared Adaptive Control Arm Works (I-SPY 2, RECOVERY): Instead of running 5 separate trials with 5 redundant placebo groups, an Adaptive Platform Trial compares multiple experimental drugs simultaneously against one shared Standard-of-Care control group.
  • Updating the Control Arm ("Graduation"): Ineffective drugs are dropped early at interim checks; when an experimental drug proves superior ("graduates"), it replaces the old control arm to become the new Standard of Care for all future drugs!
Contrast: Basket = Many Organs → 1 Mutation/Drug; Umbrella = 1 Organ → Many Mutations/Drugs; Platform = 1 Shared Evolving Control Arm.

Gene Therapy Trial Design • Ethical & Clinical Constraints

Why CRISPR Trials Cannot Use a Double-Blind Placebo

Why sham myeloablative chemotherapy or sham brain surgery is ethically prohibited in human gene therapy.
Slide 34
🧬 When Is an Open-Label Single-Arm Trial Necessary?
  • Toxic Conditioning Regimen: In autologous (self-derived) CRISPR therapy (Casgevy, NCT03745287), patients receive myeloablative busulfan chemotherapy, high-dose conditioning that destroys existing bone marrow before infusing edited CD34+ stem cells.
  • Prohibition on Fatal Sham Procedures: Wiping out a placebo patient's bone marrow with busulfan and infusing unedited saline would be fatal and unethical.
  • Deterministic Natural History: In untreated infantile SMA Type 1 (Zolgensma), $0\%$ of babies ever sit unassisted—so when 11 of 12 treated babies sat up, no placebo group was needed.
Ethical Standard: Single-arm open-label trials replace placebo arms whenever conditioning is invasive and untreated natural history is invariant.
Ethical & Scientific Basis for Single-Arm Gene Therapy Trials No Sham Myeloablation

Single-Arm Proof in Casgevy (CLIMB SCD-121)

Study of the Week (Part 1): The POLO Pancreatic Cancer Trial

Targeting inherited BRCA1/2 DNA-repair defects in pancreatic cancer to double time before tumor regrowth.
Slide 36
🔬 Targeting Broken DNA Repair in Pancreatic Cancer
  • Disease Context: Metastatic pancreatic cancer is one of the deadliest tumors (5-year survival $<12\%$). About $5\%$ of patients inherit a broken DNA-repair gene (BRCA1 or BRCA2).
  • How Olaparib Works: Healthy cells have two ways to repair DNA breaks, but BRCA-mutant cancer cells have lost their main repair kit. Olaparib blocks the cell's backup repair protein (PARP), causing the tumor's DNA to shatter so cancer cells die while healthy cells survive.
  • The POLO Trial Result (NCT02184195): Screening $3,315$ patients identified $154$ with inherited BRCA mutations whose tumors stabilized on initial chemotherapy. Daily Olaparib pills nearly doubled the time before tumors regrew (median PFS $7.4\text{ vs. }3.8\text{ months}$, $\text{HR} = 0.53$).
Clinical Milestone: Won December 2019 FDA approval as the first genetically targeted maintenance pill for pancreatic cancer.

Study of the Week (Part 2): Why Did Median Survival Look Identical?

How placebo crossover blurred the 19-month median survival—while early Olaparib nearly doubled 3-year survival (33.9% vs. 17.8%).
Slide 37
⚖️ Unpacking the POLO Survival Puzzle (JCO 2022)
  • The Puzzle (Identical Median Survival): Even though Olaparib doubled the time before tumors regrew ($7.4\text{ vs. }3.8\text{ months}$), Median Overall Survival (when 50% of patients had died) looked identical in both groups: $19.0\text{ months}$ on Olaparib vs. $19.2\text{ months}$ on Placebo ($\text{HR} = 0.83$).
  • What Happened? (Placebo Patients Crossed Over to Active Drug): When a placebo patient's tumor started growing again around Month 4, doctors did not leave them untreated—$26\%$ of placebo patients crossed over and received Olaparib (or similar drugs) later, extending their lives and erasing the difference at Month 19!
  • The Long-Term Payoff (3-Year Survival Tail): Looking past the 19-month midpoint to Year 3 (36 months), patients who received Olaparib up front had nearly double the survival rate ($33.9\%\text{ vs. }17.8\%$) and stayed off toxic chemotherapy twice as long.
Key Takeaway: When placebo patients cross over to active treatment after progression, the 50% Median OS slice looks flat—so clinicians also check the 3-year survival tail!
