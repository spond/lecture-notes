Temporal Trends: Why Cancer Mortality Rose for 60 Years—Then Fell 34%

Tracing the 25-year lag between cigarette smoking and lung cancer, and how prevention saved 4.1 million lives.
Slide 03
📉 The 1991 Turning Point (~4.1 Million Deaths Averted)
  • The 60-Year Rise (1930–1991): U.S. age-adjusted cancer mortality climbed steadily from 143 to a peak of 215 deaths per 100,000 in 1991, driven almost entirely by mass-manufactured cigarettes.
  • The 25-Year Mutational Lag: Per-capita cigarette smoking peaked in 1963 (~4,300 cigarettes/adult/year) right before the 1964 Surgeon General's Report, but lung cancer deaths did not peak until 1990–1991 because somatic mutations take 25–30 years to evolve into lethal tumors.
  • The 34% Decline (1991–2026): Since 1991, the overall U.S. cancer death rate has dropped by 34%, averting over 4.1 million cancer deaths.
  • Three Pillars of the Decline: (1) Smoking cessation; (2) Removing precancerous polyps via colonoscopy and cervical lesions via Pap/HPV screening; and (3) Targeted genomic & immune therapies.
Historical Triumph: Stomach cancer was the #1 U.S. cancer killer in 1930, but dropped >85% thanks to refrigeration (less salt/nitrite preservation) and treating Helicobacter pylori infections.

Movement 1 • Deep Historical Perspective

Cancer Through the Ages: Is Malignancy a "Modern" Disease?

Tracing tumors from 76-million-year-old dinosaur fossils and Egyptian papyri to the 19th-century cell theory.
Slide 06
🏛️ Four Millennia of Confronting the "Crab"
  • Ancient Fossils (76M–1.7M BC): Paleopathologists diagnosed malignant osteosarcoma (bone cancer) inside a 76-million-year-old Centrosaurus dinosaur fibula and a 1.7-million-year-old hominin toe bone—proving cancer is an ancient vulnerability of multicellular life, not a purely modern invention.
  • The Edwin Smith Papyrus (Egypt, c. 1600 BC): The oldest medical text (attributed to Imhotep) describes Case 45: bulging breast tumors cool and hard as an unripe fruit. Under therapy, the scribe wrote: "There is none."
  • Why Do We Call It "Cancer"? (400 BC–200 AD): Hippocrates coined karkinos (Greek for crab, Latinized to cancer) because the swollen veins radiating from an invasive breast tumor resembled a crab's legs dug into sand. Galen later coined onkos (swelling) but blamed tumors on trapped "black bile," halting surgery for 1,500 years.
  • The Longevity Unmasking Paradox: Cancer seemed rare in antiquity only because infectious diseases killed most humans by age 30. Once sanitation, vaccines, and antibiotics doubled life expectancy to ~79, millions of people lived into the 50–85 age window where $\text{Age}^6$ somatic evolution peaks!
Rudolf Virchow (1858): Using the microscope, Virchow proved "omnis cellula e cellula" (every cell arises from a pre-existing cell)—proving tumors are rogue dividing human cells, not "black bile"!

⏳ Four Paradigm Shifts That Built Modern Oncology
  • 1. Environmental Carcinogens & Tumor Viruses (1775–1911): British surgeon Percivall Pott (1775) discovered that coal soot caused scrotal cancer in child chimney sweeps—the first proof that an external chemical causes cancer. Movement 2 • The Somatic Theory of Cancer

Germline vs. Somatic: Why 90% of Cancers Are Acquired Inside One Organ

Distinguishing mutations present in the fertilized egg from mutations acquired during adult tissue maintenance.
Slide 08
🧬 Two Origins of Disease-Causing DNA Variants
  • 1. Germline Variants (<10% of Cancers): Passed from parent to child via sperm or egg. Present in 100% of body cells (detectable in a cheek swab or blood draw) and can be passed to future children (e.g., hereditary BRCA1 or RB1 syndromes).
  • 2. Somatic Mutations (>90% of Cancers): Acquired inside a single body cell (such as one lung epithelial cell or one colon stem cell) during post-natal life. Absent from healthy blood or normal organs, and never passed to offspring.
  • The Boveri & Muller Foundation (1914–1927): Theodor Boveri first proposed that abnormal chromosomes drive tumors, and Hermann Muller proved X-rays mutate DNA—establishing that cancer is fundamentally a genetic disease at the cellular level, even when it is not hereditary!
  • Clinical Paired Sequencing: To find somatic tumor mutations, clinics sequence both the tumor biopsy AND normal blood, subtracting the patient's inherited germline variants.
Core Distinction: All cancers are genetic (driven by mutated DNA inside tumor cells), but fewer than 10% are hereditary (passed down through families).

Movement 2 • The 1954 Mathematical Breakthrough

Armitage & Doll (1954): Counting Cancer Mutations Before DNA Sequencing

How a straight line on a log-log mortality graph proved that a single mutation is never enough to cause cancer.
Slide 09
📐 The Multi-Stage Theory of Carcinogenesis (1954)
  • The "One-Hit" Failure: In the early 1950s, scientists wondered if a single random mutation turns a normal cell cancerous. If 1 hit were enough (at constant annual rate), cancer incidence would stay flat across all adult ages.
  • The Power-Law Discovery: In 1954, British statisticians Peter Armitage and Richard Doll (Br J Cancer) plotted epithelial cancer death rates against age $t$ and discovered that incidence climbs with the 5th to 6th power of age ($I(t) \propto t^{k-1}$).
  • The Log-Log Straight Line: Taking the logarithm of both axes turns the curve into a straight line whose slope equals $k - 1$, where $k$ is the number of independent rate-limiting steps required: $$\log(\text{Incidence}) = (k - 1)\log(\text{Age}) + \text{constant}$$
  • Counting the Steps ($k = 6\text{ to }7$): Because colon, stomach, and pancreas cancers exhibited a log-log slope of $\sim 5\text{ to }6$, Armitage and Doll deduced—23 years before DNA sequencing—that a cell must accumulate 6 to 7 sequential hits to become malignant!
Quantitative Proof: Doubling your age from 35 to 70 ($2\times$) multiplies your annual risk of a 6-hit epithelial cancer by roughly $2^5 = 32\text{-fold}$ to $2^6 = 64\text{-fold}$!

Movement 2 • The Stem Cell Replication Hypothesis

Tomasetti & Vogelstein (2015): Why Do Some Organs Get Cancer 50× More Often?

Plotting lifetime cancer risk against total stem-cell divisions across 31 human tissues ($R = 0.81$).
Slide 10
🎲 The Anatomy Puzzle & Replicative ("R") Mutations
  • The Anatomical Paradox: The small intestine and large intestine (colon) are exposed to the same digested food, yet colon cancer (~4.8% lifetime risk) is 24× more common than small-intestine cancer (~0.2%), and bone or brain cancers are rarer still (<0.1%).
  • Counting Stem Cell Divisions (Science 2015 & 2017): Cristian Tomasetti and Bert Vogelstein calculated the total number of lifetime stem cell divisions ($l_{\text{total}}$) across 31 human tissue types and plotted them against lifetime cancer risk.
  • The 0.81 Correlation ($R^2 = 0.65$): Across 5 orders of magnitude, lifetime stem cell divisions correlated strongly with tissue cancer risk ($r = 0.81$, $p < 10^{-7}$), explaining ~65% of the variance in cancer risk between different organs!
  • Three Mutation Sources (R, E, H): Every time a stem cell divides to renew a tissue, human DNA polymerases make ~3 random copying typos (Replicative or "R" mutations), alongside Environmental ("E") and Hereditary ("H") hits.
Biological Mechanism: Because colon stem cells divide ~73 times per year ($10^{12}$ total lifetime divisions) while brain or bone cells rarely divide, the colon rolls the polymerase mutation dice thousands of times more often!

Movement 2 • Statistical Literacy & Public Health

Resolving the "Bad Luck" Firestorm: Variance vs. Preventability

Why explaining 65% of tissue-to-tissue variance does NOT mean 65% of human cancers are unpreventable.
Slide 11
⚖️ What Tomasetti & Vogelstein Got Right—and What Headlines Confused
  • The Media Headline Trap (2015): Newspapers Worldwide reported "Two-Thirds of Cancers Are Just Bad Luck—Healthy Living Won't Help!", sparking fierce rebuttals from the WHO and epidemiologists (Wu et al., Nature 2016).
  • The Statistical Distinction ($R^2$ vs. Preventable Cases): Finding that stem-cell turnover explains 65% of why the colon gets more cancer than the bone ($R^2 = 0.65$) measures relative differences between organs, not how many total cancers can be prevented!
  • The Multi-Hit Multiplication Rule: Because a tumor requires 5 to 7 sequential hits (Slide 7), a single tumor can have 3 Replicative ("R") mutations AND 2 Environmental ("E") mutations. Blocking just the 2 environmental hits prevents the tumor completely!
  • The Consensus Numbers (2017 Follow-Up): Roughly 66% of individual driver mutations arise from endogenous replication errors (explaining why lifelong non-smokers and children still get cancer), while ~40%–45% of total cancer deaths are preventable via lifestyle, vaccines (HPV/HBV), and screening.
Clinical Compassion & Prevention: This framework removes guilt from patients who did everything right and still developed cancer, while proving why avoiding mutagens stops multi-hit progression.

Movement 3 • Somatic Darwinian Evolution

Nowell (1976): Clonal Expansion, Selective Sweeps, and Branching Trees

Why a tumor is not a uniform bag of identical cells, but a branching evolutionary tree of competing subclones.
Slide 13
🌳 Peter Nowell's Clonal Evolution Model (Science 1976)
  • Philadelphia Anchor (UPenn, 1976): Co-discoverer of the Philadelphia chromosome, Peter Nowell synthesized evolutionary biology and oncology, proposing that tumors evolve by somatic natural selection.
  • Clonal Sweeps: When a single cell acquires a mutation that speeds up division or resists low oxygen, its daughter cells out-reproduce neighboring cells in a clonal sweep, taking over the niche.
  • Trunk vs. Branch Mutations:
    • Trunk (Clonal) Mutations: Acquired in the original founder cell; present in 100% of tumor cells across every region of the tumor.
    • Branch (Subclonal) Mutations: Acquired later as the tumor grows; present only in regional sub-populations (e.g., 15% or 30% of cells).
  • Therapy as an Evolutionary Bottleneck: Chemotherapy or targeted drugs wipe out sensitive branches, clearing space for a pre-existing resistant branch to repopulate the tumor!
Clinical Strategy: The best precision drugs target Trunk mutations (shared by 100% of tumor cells); targeting a single Branch mutation leaves the rest of the tree alive to relapse.

Movement 3 • Signal vs. Noise in the Cancer Genome

Drivers vs. Passengers: Finding 5 Needles in a 20,000-Mutation Haystack

Why 99.9% of mutations inside a tumor are harmless hitchhikers—and how evolutionary math exposes the culprits.
Slide 14
🎯 Separating Causal Culprits from Neutral Hitchhikers
  • The Sequencing Shock: Whole-genome sequencing of a typical lung or melanoma tumor reveals 10,000 to 50,000 somatic mutations, yet only 4 to 8 are actually causing the cancer!
  • Driver Mutations ($s > 0$): Somatic alterations that confer a physical survival or growth advantage (positive selection coefficient $s > 0$), directly propelling clonal expansion (e.g., KRAS G12D, TP53 R175H).
  • Passenger Mutations ($s \approx 0$): Neutral DNA typos (>99.9% of all tumor mutations) that happened to be present in the cell before or when a Driver mutation swept the clone to fixation (genetic hitchhiking).
  • How Bioinformaticians Spot Drivers ($dN/dS > 1$): Compare the rate of protein-altering non-synonymous ($dN$) mutations to silent synonymous ($dS$) mutations across thousands of patients. Passenger genes sit at $dN/dS \approx 1.0$ (random noise), whereas true Driver genes show $dN/dS \gg 1$ (up to 10–50× enrichment!).
The "Giant Gene" Trap: Huge genes like TTN (Titin, 109,000 bp) or olfactory receptors are mutated in 40% of tumors purely because they are massive targets for random Passenger hits!

Movement 3 • Spatial Heterogeneity & Diagnostics

The Single-Biopsy Blind Spot: Why One Needle Misses 65% of the Tumor

Gerlinger et al. (NEJM 2012) proved that different corners of the same tumor carry different mutations.
Slide 15
🧭 Multi-Region Sequencing (Gerlinger et al., NEJM 2012)
  • The Experiment: Charles Swanton's team took a single surgically removed kidney tumor (renal cell carcinoma), sliced it into 10 spatially distinct regions (R1–R10) plus metastases, and sequenced each piece separately.
  • The Shocking Result (65% Branched): Only 34% of somatic mutations were shared across every biopsy (Trunk hits like VHL). Fully 65% of mutations were restricted to specific regions!
  • Good vs. Bad Prognosis in the Same Mass: Biopsy R4 carried a gene signature predicting "favorable survival," while Biopsy R9—just 3 cm away in the exact same tumor—carried a signature predicting "rapid lethal progression."
  • The Liquid Biopsy Fix (ctDNA): Because dying cells from every region of the primary tumor and distant metastases shed circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) into the bloodstream, a single tube of blood captures the whole evolutionary tree!
Clinical Takeaway: A single needle biopsy is like photographing one street corner and assuming the whole city looks identical; ctDNA liquid biopsy pools DNA shed from every tumor neighborhood.

Your Healthy Skin & Esophagus Are Already a Patchwork of Mutant Clones

Martincorena et al. (Science 2015 / 2018) discovered that healthy middle-aged organs teem with cancer driver mutations.
Slide 16
🧩 The Healthy Tissue Shock (Martincorena & Campbell)

Movement 4 • Functional Architecture of Cancer Drivers

Gas Pedals and Brakes: Oncogenes vs. Tumor Suppressor Genes

Why turning a growth switch ON takes one precise hit, while disabling a safety brake requires two hits.
Slide 17
🏎️ The Two Classes of Protein-Coding Driver Genes
  • 1. Proto-Oncogenes → Oncogenes ("Stuck Gas Pedals"):
    • Normal Job: Proto-oncogenes encode proteins that tell a cell to divide only when an external growth factor arrives (e.g., KRAS, BRAF, EGFR, MYC, ERBB2/HER2).
    • Mutant State: A gain-of-function mutation locks the protein permanently ON even with zero growth signal.
    • Allele Math: Dominant (1 Hit required)—jamming 1 of your 2 gas pedals to the floor makes the car accelerate!
  • 2. Tumor Suppressor Genes ("Cut Brake Lines"):
    • Normal Job: Halt division, repair damaged DNA, or trigger cell suicide (e.g., TP53, RB1, APC, BRCA1/2, PTEN).
    • Mutant State: Loss-of-function mutations destroy or truncate the protein.
    • Allele Math: Recessive at the cellular level (2 Hits required)—a car has two brake circuits (maternal & paternal allele); you must cut both before the brakes fail!
Druggability Rule: It is chemically much easier to design a pill that blocks an overactive Oncogene (e.g., BRAF or EGFR inhibitor) than a pill that restores a deleted Tumor Suppressor protein!

Movement 4 • Molecular Mechanisms of Oncogenes

Three Ways to Jam the Gas Pedal—and How to Read Lollipop Plots

Comparing focal missense hotspots in oncogenes (KRAS/BRAF) against scattered truncations in tumor suppressors.
Slide 18
🔥 Three Molecular Routes to Activate an Oncogene
  • 1. Active-Site Point Mutation (Hotspot): A single amino acid swap disables the protein's "OFF" switch.
    • Example 1: KRAS p.G12D / p.G12C (Codon 12) blocks GTP hydrolysis, locking KRAS permanently bound to GTP ("ON") in 90% of pancreatic and 40% of colon cancers.
    • Example 2: BRAF p.V600E mimics phosphorylation in 50% of melanomas.
  • 2. Gene Copy Amplification (50× Dosage): The cell duplicates a normal proto-oncogene into 20–100 copies (often on extrachromosomal circular DNA / ecDNA), flooding the cell membrane with receptors (e.g., ERBB2 / HER2 in breast cancer; MYCN in neuroblastoma).
  • 3. Chromosomal Translocation (Chimeric Fusion): Two chromosomes break and swap arms, gluing an always-on promoter or dimerization domain onto a kinase (e.g., $t(9;22)$ Philadelphia Chromosome fusing BCR-ABL1 in Chronic Myeloid Leukemia).
Visual Diagnostic Rule (cBioPortal Lollipop Plots): A single tall spike at 1–2 codons = Oncogene; mutations scattered evenly across the entire gene length = Tumor Suppressor!

Movement 4 • Tumor Suppressors & Two-Hit Kinetics

Knudson's Two-Hit Hypothesis (1971): Sporadic vs. Familial Retinoblastoma

How a pediatrician used age-at-diagnosis curves in childhood eye cancer to discover tumor suppressor genes.
Slide 19
👁️ Alfred Knudson's Retinoblastoma Deduction (PNAS 1971)
  • The Clinical Puzzle: Retinoblastoma is a childhood retinal tumor caused by losing both copies of the RB1 tumor suppressor on chromosome 13q14. Why do some children get multiple tumors in both eyes as infants, while others get a single tumor in one eye as toddlers?
  • Sporadic Form (60%): The child is born with two intact alleles (RB1+/+). A single retinal cell must suffer two independent somatic hits ($P \propto t^2$)—a rare coincidence causing unilateral (one-eye) disease around ~30 months.
  • Familial Form (40%): Via fertilization, the child inherits Hit #1 in the germline (RB1+/- in all $3 \times 10^6$ retinal cells). Only one somatic hit ($P \propto t^1$) is needed, causing bilateral, multifocal tumors by ~14 months.
  • The Pedigree Paradox: At the cellular level, RB1 loss is recessive (both alleles must fail). At the family pedigree level, risk appears autosomal dominant (~90% penetrance) because 1 somatic hit among $3 \times 10^6$ dividing cells is practically guaranteed!

How the Second Brake Line Snaps: Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH)

Why losing the second wild-type copy happens 100× faster than waiting for a second independent point typo.
Slide 20
✂️ Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH) & Epigenetic Silencing
  • Defining LOH: Once a cell is heterozygous for a mutant tumor suppressor (+/-), Loss of Heterozygosity (LOH) is the chromosomal elimination or replacement of the remaining wild-type (+) allele.
  • 1. Hemizygous Chromosomal Deletion (CN = 1): A double-strand break deletes the wild-type chromosome arm (e.g., del(17p) for TP53 or del(13q) for RB1), leaving the cell with only its single mutant allele (-/0).
  • 2. Copy-Neutral LOH (Mitotic Recombination / UPD): During mitosis, homologous chromosomes cross over so the daughter cell inherits two identical copies of the mutant segment (-/-). Total copy number stays 2.0, fooling read-depth-only assays!
  • 3. Promoter CpG Hypermethylation (Epigenetic Hit #2): DNA methyltransferases coat the wild-type promoter in $5\text{mC}$ methyl groups, silencing transcription without changing a single DNA letter (classic in MLH1 and BRCA1).
Diagnostic Blind Spot: Standard exome read-depth misses Copy-Neutral LOH (needs SNP B-allele frequency) and Promoter Methylation (needs methylation-specific testing or immunohistochemistry).

TP53 Tetramer Poisoning ($15/16$) & Peto's Paradox in Elephants

Why the most mutated tumor suppressor has missense hotspots—and why elephants rarely get cancer.
Slide 21
🐘 TP53 ("Guardian of the Genome") & Peto's Paradox
  • Master Emergency Brake: Mutated in ~50% of all human cancers, TP53 encodes transcription factor p53, which senses DNA damage and activates CDKN1A (p21) to halt division or BAX/PUMA to trigger apoptosis.
  • Why TP53 Has Missense Hotspots (R175H, R248Q, R273H): Unlike RB1 or APC, p53 functions as a 4-subunit homotetramer. In a heterozygous +/- cell, a single mutant subunit incorporates into the complex and poisons the whole tetramer—a dominant-negative effect where $1 - (1/2)^4 = 15/16$ ($93.75\%$) of tetramers are crippled before Hit #2!
  • Li-Fraumeni Syndrome: Germline TP53 inheritance causes a ~90% lifetime risk of childhood sarcomas, brain, breast, and adrenal tumors.
  • Peto's Paradox in Elephants (Abegglen/Lynch 2015): Elephants have 100× more cells than humans yet <5% cancer mortality (vs. ~20% in humans) because they evolved 20 copies (40 alleles) of TP53, triggering instant apoptosis of damaged cells!
Mathematical Takeaway: In a heterozygous TP53+/- cell (50% WT, 50% Mutant monomers), only $(0.5)^4 = \mathbf{1/16\ (6.25\%)}$ of assembled 4-mers are pure wild-type!

Every Mutagen Leaves a Scar: The 96-Channel COSMIC Spectrum

Why 20,000 passenger mutations are not useless trash—they are a high-resolution forensic recording of what damaged the DNA.
Slide 23
🔍 Combinatorial Math of Mutational Signatures (Alexandrov & Stratton, 2013)
  • Why Passengers Are Forensic Gold: While a single Driver mutation tells you which brake broke, the thousands of neutral Passenger mutations across the genome record what chemical or enzymatic process caused the DNA damage.
  • Why 6 Base Substitutions (Not 12)? Because DNA is a complementary double helix, a $G>A$ change on one strand is identical to a $C>T$ change on the partner strand. By convention, we always name the mutated pyrimidine ($C$ or $T$): $C>A,\ C>G,\ C>T,\ T>A,\ T>C,\ T>G$ (6 classes).
  • Why Exactly 96 Channels? Mutagens care about the immediate $5'$ upstream base (4 choices: $A,C,G,T$) and $3'$ downstream base (4 choices: $A,C,G,T$): $$\text{Channels} = 6 \text{ substitutions} \times 4 \text{ (5' bases)} \times 4 \text{ (3' bases)} = \mathbf{96 \text{ channels}}$$
  • Unmixing the Crime Scene (NMF): Non-negative Matrix Factorization decomposes a patient's tumor catalog $\vec{M}$ into weighted COSMIC Single Base Substitution (SBS) signatures ($\vec{M} \approx \sum w_k \vec{S}_k$).
Key Equation: $6 \text{ pyrimidine base changes} \times 4 \text{ upstream bases} \times 4 \text{ downstream bases} = \mathbf{96 \text{ trinucleotide bars}}$.

Exogenous Crime Scenes: UV Radiation (SBS7) vs. Tobacco Smoke (SBS4)

☀️🚬 Chemical Lesions Dictate the 96-Bar Fingerprint
  • COSMIC SBS7a/b — Ultraviolet (UV) Light in Melanoma: UV photons covalently fuse adjacent pyrimidine bases ($TT, TC, CT, CC$) into bulky rings called cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers (CPDs). Endogenous Crime Scenes: The Aging Clock (SBS1) & APOBEC (SBS2/13)

Why human DNA mutates steadily even in complete darkness with zero environmental toxins.
Slide 25
⏳⚡ Water Chemistry (SBS1) & Antiviral Friendly Fire (SBS2/13)
  • COSMIC SBS1 — The Universal Mitotic Aging Clock: In human DNA, cytosine followed by guanine ($5'\text{-CpG-}3'$) is usually methylated into 5-methylcytosine ($5\text{mC}$). Normal $C$ deaminates in water to Uracil ($U$), which repair enzymes immediately spot as illegal in DNA. However, $5\text{mC}$ deaminates directly into Thymine ($T$)—a legal DNA base!   • COSMIC SBS2 & SBS13 — APOBEC3 "Friendly Fire": The APOBEC3 cytidine deaminase family evolved to destroy invading viruses (like HIV and HPV) by mutating viral single-stranded DNA.   • Kataegis ("Thunderstorm"): When APOBEC attacks a long stretch of single-stranded DNA at a chromosome break, it creates a dense cluster of hundreds of mutations within a few kilobases.
Chemical Insight: Deaminating normal Cytosine makes Uracil (caught instantly); deaminating methylated $5\text{mC}$ at CpG makes Thymine (creating the 4 clockwork spikes of SBS1).

Macro-Genomic Earthquakes: Chromothripsis & Whole-Genome Doubling

Not all cancer evolution is gradual—sometimes a single chromosome shatters into 200 pieces in one afternoon.
Slide 27
💥 Chromothripsis ("Chromosome Shattering", Stephens et al. 2011)
  • Punctuated Equilibrium in Cancer: While Armitage & Doll's model tracks gradual decade-by-decade point mutations, ~30–40% of human tumors experience a single catastrophic structural event that rewires a chromosome overnight.
  • The Micronucleus Trap: During an abnormal mitosis, a lagging chromosome is left behind outside the main nucleus inside a fragile micronucleus. When the micronuclear membrane ruptures, cytoplasmic enzymes pulverize that single chromosome into 50–1,000 shards.
  • Sloppy Stitching & ecDNA Rings: Non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) stitches the surviving shards together in random order—deleting multiple tumor suppressors at once and circularizing oncogenes into extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) rings!
  • Whole-Genome Doubling (WGD): Seen in ~30% of solid tumors ($2n \to 4n$), buffering the tumor against lethal deletions.
Circos Plot Signature: Unlike gradual instability (scattered across all 23 chromosomes), chromothripsis concentrates 100+ crisscrossing rearrangement arcs onto just 1 or 2 chromosomes oscillating between $\text{CN}=1$ and $\text{CN}=2$.
